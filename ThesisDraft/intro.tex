\section{Motivations and  Project Aims}

It is hard to overstate the importance of spectra in applied mathematics. Trefethen notes that at least twenty Nobel Prizes in Physics have been awarded for phenomena associated with eigenvalues \cite{NickEssay}.
Spectra play a major role in numerous fields, such as acoustics, chemical analysis, dynamical systems, fluid mechanics, quantum mechanics, and partial differential equations\footnote{For an overview of the history and applications of eigenvalue problems, which are a subset of spectral problems, see the introduction of \cite{Nick} and the references therein.}. A particularly interesting example can be found in spectroscopy. As displayed in \Cref{spec pic}, the black `Fraunhofer lines' that appear in spectroscopic images of the sun's light correspond to the differences of eigenvalues of the Schrödinger operator for atoms such as H, Fe, Ca, Na, and Mg \cite{Nick}. Spectroscopic measurements such as these allowed for the measurement of redshifts of distant galaxies, leading to the discovery of the expanding universe \cite{Nick}.

Although the importance of spectra is beyond question, their reliable computation can be challenging \cite{Forward}. In finite dimensions, we compute the spectrum of a matrix, which is equivalent to the set of its eigenvalues.  In particular, if $B \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ then its spectrum is the following set of points:
\begin{equation} \label{matrix spec}
    \operatorname{Sp}(B) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: \det (B-zI) = 0 \} = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (B-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist} \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity matrix. 
% One might be tempted to think that this is a straightforward problem. However, as we will see, even for non-normal matrices, computed eigenvalues may be unreliable. 
In infinite dimensions, we consider a linear operator $A$ on a Hilbert space $\H,$ whose spectrum is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{SpA}
    \operatorname{Sp}(A) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (A-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist as a bounded operator on } \H \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity operator. 

A point $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if there exists a nonzero $u$ such that $Au=z u.$ Equivalently, $z$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ when $A-z I$ is not injective. In contrast to finite dimensions, $A -zI$ can fail to be boundedly invertible for values of $z$ that are not eigenvalues of $A.$ For example, $A-zI$ may be injective but not surjective. Thus, the
spectrum of a linear operator includes the set of eigenvalues of $A,$ but is generally much richer.

The standard approach for computing the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator is often referred to as the \textit{discretise-then-solve} approach \cite[Chapter~1]{Matt}.

This approach consists of discretising the operator with a finite-dimensional matrix, computing the spectrum of the discretised operator, and hoping that the spectrum of the discretised operator approaches the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator as the size of the discretisation increases. Despite being a successful approach for many problems, it is fraught with difficulties. If the problem is not discretised very carefully, \textit{spectral pollution} can occur, where a sequence of discretised operators $,A_n,$ has a corresponding sequence of eigenvalues, $\lambda_n \in \Sp{A_n},$ such that $ \lambda_n \rightarrow \lambda \notin \Sp{A}$ as $n \rightarrow \infty.$

Under certain conditions on the operator, Colbrook's algorithm yields a convergent approximation of the spectrum that captures the continuous components of the spectrum and does not suffer from spectral pollution or spectral invisibility. This success, however, comes at a significant computational cost because it requires solving eigenproblems at potentially many points in the complex plane. Furthermore, as we shall see, coarse discretisations of the folded operators can produce poor approximations of the bound on the distance to the spectrum. 
As a result, further refinement, and thus more expensive computations, may be needed to obtain meaningful approximations.

In this dissertation, we explore methods for increasing the efficiency of Colbrook's algorithm for computing the pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators. We first aim to exploit the structure of the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm in an effort to solve them efficiently. To do so, we apply the \textit{dual-weighted residual} (DWR) method to estimate the error in the quantities used to compute the spectrum. This method uses the solution of the adjoint, or dual, problem to estimate the error in a computed quantity of interest, rather than in the computed solution itself \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline, Becker}. In our setting, the quantity of interest is the eigenvalue rather than the corresponding eigenfunction. The resulting eigenvalue error estimates are used both to improve the computed approximations and to drive adaptive refinement of the meshes used to discretise the auxiliary eigenproblems.
 We refer to this process, in which we adapt the mesh used at each test point in the complex plane, as ``inner adaptivity''.

The remainder of the dissertation is structured as follows. 
In Chapter 2, we present all relevant background material for our methodology. We begin with an overview of the spectra and pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators. We then introduce the standard discretise-then-solve approach and discuss its common pitfalls. As an example, we demonstrate spectral pollution when a nai\"ve finite element discretisation is applied to the Maxwell eigenproblem. This motivates Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra, which we first describe in its abstract formulation and then in the finite element setting. By applying the finite element realisation of Colbrook's algorithm to the Maxwell eigenproblem, we show that, although the algorithm avoids the pitfalls the discretise-then-solve approach, its accuracy critically depends on the quality of the underlying discretisation. As a result, we introduce the DWR method

and explore its application to our finite element realisation of Colbrook's algorithm. 

In Chapter 3, we develop our adaptive methodology. We first construct an adaptive mesh refinement technique for the auxiliary eigenproblems arising in Colbrook's algorithm. We then develop an approach for adaptively selecting test points within it. Chapter 3 ends with our main new algorithm, which couples these two levels of adaptivity. 

In Chapter 4, we apply our adaptive algorithm to a one-dimensional Dirac-type operator and to the Maxwell eigenproblem. In Chapter 5, we summarise our work and discuss some potential directions for future research.
