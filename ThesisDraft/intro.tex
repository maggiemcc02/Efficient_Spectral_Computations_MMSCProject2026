\section{Motivations and  Project Aims}

With at least twenty Nobel Prizes in Physics coming from phenomena associated with eigenvalues, and thus with spectra, it is hard to overstate the importance of spectra in applied mathematics \cite{NickEssay}. Spectra play a major role in numerous fields, such as acoustics, chemical analysis, dynamical systems, fluid mechanics, quantum mechanics, and partial differential equations (PDEs)\footnote{For an overview of the history and applications of eigenvalue problems, which are a subset of spectral problems, see the introduction of \cite{Nick} and the references therein.}. A particularly interesting example can be found in spectroscopy. As displayed in Figure \ref{spec pic}, the black `Fraunhofer lines' that appear in spectroscopic images of the sun's light correspond to the differences of eigenvalues of the Schrödinger operator for atoms such as H, Fe, Ca, Na, and Mg \cite{Nick}. Spectroscopic measurements such as these allowed for the measurement of redshifts of distant galaxies, leading to the discovery of the expanding universe \cite{Nick}.

Although the importance of spectra is beyond question, their reliable computation is another matter \cite{Forward}. In finite dimensions, we compute the spectrum of a matrix, which is equivalent to the set of its eigenvalues.  In particular, if $B \in \mathbb{C}^{nxn}$ then its spectrum is the following set of points:
\begin{equation} \label{matrix spec}
    \operatorname{Sp}(B) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: \det (B-zI) = 0 \} = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (B-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist} \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity matrix. 
% One might be tempted to think that this is a straightforward problem. However, as we will see, even for non-normal matrices, computed eigenvalues may be unreliable. 
In infinite dimensions, we consider a linear operator $A$ on a Hilbert space $H,$ whose spectrum is defined by the following set:
\begin{equation} \label{SpA}
    \operatorname{Sp}(A) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (A-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist as a bounded operator on } H \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity operator. The spectrum of a linear operator includes the set of eigenvalues of $A,$ but is generally much richer. This follows from the fact that in infinite dimensions there are more ways for $A-zI$ to fail to have a bounded inverse \cite{Matt}. As a result, new phenomena are present in the spectra of linear operators that make them much harder to reliably compute. To see this, consider the right-shift operator, 
\begin{equation}\label{right shift}
    A(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots),
\end{equation}
that acts on square-summable sequences\footnote{Claiming that a sequence is `square-summable' is claiming that the sequence is an element of 
    $l^2(\mathbb{N}) = \{z = \{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N{}}}: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |x_n|^2 < \infty\}.$}.This operator's spectrum is equal to the unit disc, but it has no eigenvalues\footnote{For more information on this example, see Appendix ?}.

The standard approach for computing the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator is often referred to as the \textit{discretize-then-solve} approach \cite{Matt}. This approach consists of discretizing the operator with a finite-dimensional matrix, computing the spectrum of the discretized operator, and hoping that the spectrum of the discretized operator approaches the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator as the size of the discretization increases. Despite being a successful approach for many problems, it is fraught with difficulties. If the problem is not discretized very carefully, \textit{spectral pollution} can occur, where the discretized operator has eigenvalues that converge to something not in the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator. Furthermore, the approach can suffer from \textit{spectral invisibility}, where discretizing the operator causes us to miss parts of the spectrum, even as the size of the discretization increases. Even if our discretization manages to avoid both spectral pollution and spectral invisibility, there remains a far more catastrophic limitation of the discretize-then-solve approach. In particular, since the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators may include more than just eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, there may be parts of the spectrum that we can never capture with the finite, discrete set of eigenvalues that results from discretization \cite{Matt}. 

These drawbacks of the discretize-then-solve approach motivate the development of alternative methods for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. This challenge has been taken up in the recent work of Matthew Colbrook at Cambridge University \cite{Matt}. His work is based on the idea that infinite-dimensional problems should not be reduced too quickly and uncritically to finite-dimensional problems. Although discretization is unavoidable in the end, Colbrook shows that when and how it is introduced is crucial to the successful computation of spectra \cite{Forward}. 


% Rather than discretizing the operator at the outset and then computing the eigenvalues of the resulting matrix, 
Colbrook's algorithm considers a sequence of complex numbers $z$ drawn from a grid of points in the complex plane and computes bounds on the distance from $z$ to $\operatorname{Sp}(A).$ As we shall see, these bounds are obtained from the smallest spectral values of the \textit{folded operators} $(A-zI)^*(A-zI)$ and $(A-zI)(A-zI)^*,$ where $(A-zI)^*$ is the adjoint operator of $A-zI.$ Discretization enters Colbrook's algorithm at this auxiliary stage, where the smallest spectral values of the folded operators are approximated by the smallest eigenvalues of their finite-dimensional discretizations. The benefit of discretizing the folded operators as opposed to the original operator is that when $A$ is closed and densely defined, the folded operators are non-negative and self-adjoint.
The smallest spectral value of a non-negative self-adjoint operator is typically much easier to compute than other parts of its spectrum, and is less prone to the pitfalls of the discretize-than-solve approach \cite{Matt}. 

Under certain conditions on the operator, Colbrook's algorithm yields a convergent approximation of the spectrum that captures the continuous components of the spectrum and does not suffer from spectral pollution or spectral invisibility. This success, however, comes at a significant computational cost, since it requires solving eigenproblems at potentially many points in the complex plane. Furthermore, as we shall see, coarse discretizations of the folded operators can produce poor bounds on the distance to the spectrum, requiring further refinement and thereby increasing the cost of meaningful estimates.


In this thesis, we explore methods for increasing the efficiency of Colbrook's algorithm for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. We first aim to exploit the structure of the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm in an effort to solve them efficiently. To do so, we apply the \textit{dual-weighted residual} (DWR) method to estimate the error in the quantities used to bound the distance to the spectrum. These estimates are used to both improve the computed bounds and to drive adaptive refinement of the auxiliary eigenproblems. 

In an effort to reduce the number of eigenproblems that must be solved, we also develop a method for adaptively selecting grid points in the complex plane. This ``outer-adaptivity" balances the DWR error estimate with the desired spatial resolution of a pseudospectral computation. 

To investigate the effectiveness of our methodology, we apply it numerical examples implemented using the finite element method. In doing so, we aim to show that pairing Colbrook's algorithm with an adaptive methodology enables reliable and efficient computations of the pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators.
