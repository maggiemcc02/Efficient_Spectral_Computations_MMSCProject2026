\chapter{The Spectra of the Shift Operators} \label{Shift Appendix}


In this appendix, we explore the spectra of the shift operators. To do so, we consider the sequences $x \defeq \{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ and $y \defeq \{y_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in the $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ sequence space. This is a Hilbert space with norm satisfying $\| x \|_{\ell^2(\mathbb{N})}^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |x_n|^2$ and with inner product $( x, y )_{\ell^2(\mathbb{N})} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} x_n \overline{y_n}.$
Define the left and right shift operators on $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ as 
\begin{equation}
    L(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots) \,\, \text{ and } \,\, R(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots),
\end{equation}
respectively. 

Notice that $L^* = R,$ and that their operator norms satisfy $\| R \| = \|L\| = 1.$
When an operator $A$ is bounded, we have that 
 $\operatorname{Sp}(A) \subset \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le \|A\| \}$ \cite{krey}.
Thus,
 $$ \operatorname{Sp}(L),  \operatorname{Sp}(R) \subset \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le 1 \}.$$


 The eigenvalues of $L$ are found by solving $Lx = \lambda x$ for a $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and a nonzero $x \in \ell^2(\mathbb{N}).$
    Thus, the eigenvectors are of the form
    \begin{equation}
        x = x_1 (1, \lambda, \lambda^2, \dots),
    \end{equation}
    and must satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        |x_1|^2 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |\lambda|^{2n} < \infty.
    \end{equation}
 From this we find that
 \begin{equation}
     \Gamma(L) \defeq \text{set of eigenvalues of } L = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} \mid |\lambda| < 1 \} \subset \operatorname{Sp}(L).
 \end{equation}


Since the spectrum of a bounded operator is closed, the closed disk must also be in $\operatorname{Sp}(L)$ \cite{krey}. Thus,  $ \operatorname{Sp}(L) \supset \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le 1 \},$  $ \operatorname{Sp}(L) \subset \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le 1 \}$ and so
 $$ \operatorname{Sp}(L) = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le 1 \}.$$


 The eigenvalues of $R$ are found by solving $Rx = \lambda x$ for a $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and a nonzero $x \in \ell^2(\mathbb{N}).$
    In doing so, we find that the eigenvectors must satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        (0, x_1, x_2, \dots) = \lambda(x_1, x_2, \dots).
    \end{equation}
     Equivalently,
     \begin{equation}
         \lambda x_1 = 0, \,\, \lambda x_2 = x_1, \,\, \dots
     \end{equation}
    % $$ \lambda x_1 = 0, \,\, \lambda x_2 = x_1, \,\, .\dots$$
   The only solution is $x=0,$ which cannot be an eigenvector. Thus, $R$ has no eigenvalues.

   Recall that when an operator $A$ is closed and densely defined, $\operatorname{Sp}(A^*) = \{\overline{z} \mid z \in \operatorname{Sp}(A) \}$ \cite{Matt}. Here, $L$ and $R$ are bounded operators on all of $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ and $L^* = R$. Thus, 
   \begin{equation}
       \operatorname{Sp}(R) = \{\overline{z} \mid z \in \operatorname{Sp}(L) \} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} \mid |z| \le 1 \}.
   \end{equation}
   That is, the spectrum of the right shift operator is the closed unit disc.
