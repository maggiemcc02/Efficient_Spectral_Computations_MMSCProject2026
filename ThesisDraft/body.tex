\chapter{Background}

In this chapter, we present all necessary background material. We begin with an overview of relevant definitions and results from spectral theory. Then, we describe the discretize-and-solve approach, realized through the finite element method. Upon doing so, we examine the common pitfalls of this approach. To see one of these pitfalls in action, we apply the finite element discretization to the famous Maxwell eigenproblem, which exhibits spurious eigenvalues when discretized with standard piecewise linear Lagrange elements. 

Having explored the limitations of the discretize-and-solve approach, we introduce Matthew Colbrook's algorithm for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. When applied to the Maxwell eigenproblem, this approach avoids spurious eigenvalues, even when discretized with piecewise linear Lagrange elements. However, when the discretization is coarse, Colbrook's bounds on the distance to the spectrum may be poor. In an effort to quantify the error in these bounds, and to improve these bounds without extensive uniform refinement, we explore the dual-weighted-residual method for eigenproblems and investigate its application to Colbrook's algorithm. 


\section{An Introduction to the Spectra and Pseudospectra of Linear Operators}
\subsection{The Spectra of Linear Operators}


Let $H$ be a Hilbert space with inner product $(\cdot, \cdot)_{H}$ and norm $\|\cdot \|_{H}.$
We consider a linear operator $A : D(A) \rightarrow H$ defined on a subspace $D(A) \subset H.$ We use $R(A)$ and $D(A)$ to denote the domain and range of $A,$ respectively. Furthermore, we use $N(A)$ to denote the null space, or kernel, of $A.$   

We say that $A$ is a \textit{bounded} on $H$, denoted $A \in \mathcal{B}(H),$ if there exists a constant $c>0$ such that $\|Ax\|_{H} \le c \|x\|_{H}$ for all $x \in D(A).$ When a bounded linear operator's range lies on the real line or in the complex plane, it is called a \textit{bounded linear functional} \cite{krey}. 
Furthermore, we say that $A$ is \textit{boundedly invertible} if there exists a bounded operator $B:H \rightarrow D(A)$ such that $AB=I$ and $BA \subset I,$ where $I$ denotes the identity operator on $H.$ In this case, $B$ is called the \textit{inverse} of $A$ \cite{Matt}. 


We say that $A$ is \textit{closed} if and only if it has the following property: whenever $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence in $D(A)$ such that $x_n \rightarrow x$ and $A x_n \rightarrow y,$ then $x \in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$ \cite{krey}.
We note that when $A$ is closed, being boundedly invertible is equivalent to $N(A) = \{0\}$ and $R(A) = H.$\footnote{When $N(A) =\{0\}$ and $R(A) = H,$ $A^{-1}$ is defined. Since $A$ is closed so is $A^{-1}.$ Then, the Closed Graph Theorem tells us that $A^{-1}$ is bounded \cite{krey}.} 

 To define the unique \textit{adjoint operator} of $A,$ we assume that $A$ is d\textit{ensely defined}, i.e., that $D(A)$ is dense in $H.$ Furthermore, we define
\begin{equation}\label{D(A*)}
    D(A^*) = \{ w \in H : v \mapsto ( Av, w )_{H} \text{ is a bounded linear functional on D(A)}\}.
 \end{equation}
 Consider a $w \in D(A^*).$ Since $D(A)$ is dense in $H$, there exists a unique $u \in H$ such that 
 \begin{equation}
     (Av, w)_{H} = (v, w)_{H}, \qquad \forall v \in D(A).
 \end{equation}
 The adjoint of $A$ is defined as the linear operator $A^*$ that satisfies $u = A^*w.$ When $A^* = A,$ the linear operator $A$ is said to be \textit{self-adjoint}. Furthermore, when $A$ is closed and densely defined, $A^*A$ and $AA^*$ are self adjoint. In this case, if $AA^* = A^*A,$ then $A$ is called \textit{normal}.


In this setting, the spectrum of $A,$ as stated in \eqref{SpA}, can be equivantly formulated as the following set:
\begin{equation}\label{SpA2}
    \operatorname{Sp}(A) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : A-zI \text{ is not boundedly invertible}\}.
\end{equation}
If $A$ is not a closed linear operator, then $\operatorname{Sp}(A) = \mathbb{C}.$ Thus, going forward, we will consider the case in which $A$ is a closed linear operator. In this case, $\operatorname{Sp}(A)$ is a closed subset of the complex plane \cite{Matt}.  

As is the case in finite dimensions, $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is an \textit{eigenvalue} of $A$ if $A-zI$ is not injective, i.e., if $N(A-zI) \ne \{0\}.$ We use $\Gamma(A)$ to denote the set of the eigenvalues of $A.$ For each $z \in \Gamma(A),$ the corresponding nullspace of $A-zI$ is the eigenspace of $A$ corresponding to $z.$ Notice that $\Gamma(A) \subset \operatorname{Sp}(A)$ but that there may exist $z \in \operatorname{Sp}(A)\setminus \Gamma(A).$ For example, $(A-zI)$ may fail to be boundedly invertible because $R(A-zI)$ is not all of $H_2,$ even when $z$ is not an eigenvalue of $A.$

If the linear operator $A$ is closed and densely defined, the spectrum of $A$ can be related to the spectrum of its unique adjoint $A^*.$ In this case, $\operatorname{Sp}(A^*) = \{\overline{z} : z \in \operatorname{Sp}(A)\},$ where $\overline z$ denotes the complex conjugation of $z \in \mathbb{C}.$ Moreover, if $z \notin \operatorname{Sp}(A)$ then $(A-zI)^{-1}$ exists and $[(A-zI)^{-1}]^* = (A^* - \overline zI).$  With these properties in mind, every operator we will consider going forward is assumed to be linear, closed, and densely defined on a Hilbert space $H.$ 
% Furthermore, when $A$ is closed and densely defined, both $A^*A$ and $AA^*$ are self-adjoint.
% With these properties in mind, we henceforth assume that every operator A:D(A)⊂H→H under consideration is linear, closed, and densely defined.

 One of the most important tools for the analysis and computation of spectra is the resolvent operator:
\begin{equation}\label{resolvant}
    (A, z) \mapsto (A-zI)^{-1} \qquad z \notin \operatorname{Sp}(A).
\end{equation}
The norm of the resolvent provides information about the distance from $z\notin \operatorname{Sp}(A)$ to the spectrum of $A.$ In particular,
\begin{equation}
    \| (A-zI)^{-1} \|_H^{-1} \le \operatorname{dist}(z, \operatorname{Sp}(A)), 
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{dist}(z, \operatorname{Sp}(A)) = \inf_{w \in \operatorname{Sp}(A)} |z - w|
\end{equation}
If the operator $A$ is normal, we have equality:
\begin{equation}
    \| (A-zI)^{-1} \|_H^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z, \operatorname{Sp}(A)). 
\end{equation}
Hence, when $A$ is a normal operator, the norm of the resolvent can be used to characterize the spectrum of $A.$

To ask whether a $z\in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of the matrix $B \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n},$ we ask if $B-zI$ is invertible. However, for matrices with an ill-conditioned spectrum, a small perturbation from $B$ can change the answer from ``yes" to ``no". Thus, asking whether $B-zI$ is invertible is not a robust question. Instead, we may ask if $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|$ is large \cite{Nick}. This pattern of thinking leads to the analysis of the \textit{pseudospectra} of $B.$ In particular, for $\epsilon > 0,$ the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $B$ is the following set:
\begin{equation} \label{eps B}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(B) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}:\|(B-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} \le \epsilon \} = \operatorname{Cl} \left(\{z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(B-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} < \epsilon \} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Cl}(\cdot)$ denotes the closure of an inputted set \cite{Matt}. Here, we adopt the convention that if $z \in \operatorname{Sp}(B),$ then $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1} = 0,$ or $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\| = \infty$ \cite{Matt, Nick}. 

The $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $B$ can be equivalently defined as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eps stab B}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(B) = \bigcup_{E \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}, \|E\| \le \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(B+E).
\end{equation}
This second definition tells us that the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $B$ is the region in the complex plane describing how far an $\epsilon$-sized perturbation of $B$ can move its eigenvalues \cite{Matt}. In this sense, the pseudospectra of $B$ describe the sensitivity of its eigenvalues to perturbations. The pseudospectra of $B$ can therefore be used to identify the regions of the complex plane where eigenvalue computations are unreliable and where eigenvalues alone may provide an unreliable description of the underlying system.

Recall that $B$ is a normal matrix when $B^*B=BB^*,$ where $B^*$ denotes the conjugate transpose of $B.$ When $B$ is a normal matrix, $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{Sp}(B)).$ In this case, the $\epsilon$-pseudospectra of $B$ consist of closed $\epsilon$ balls about each eigenvalue of $B.$ Thus, an $\epsilon$-sized perturbation of $B$ cannot have eigenvalues that are further than an $\epsilon$-distance from the spectrum of $B.$ In this sense, the spectrum of $B$ is well-conditioned.  


In infinite dimensions, we consider a closed and densely defined  linear operator $A : D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H,$ defined on the Hilbert space $H.$ For each $\epsilon>0,$ the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $A$ is the following set:\footnote{Notice that in infinite dimensions, the the equality in \eqref{eps B} need not hold. This is because in infinite dimensions, the resolvent norm of an unbounded operator on a Hilbert space can be constant on open sets \cite{Matt}.}
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(A) = \operatorname{Cl}\left( \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H}^{-1} < \epsilon \}  \right).
\end{equation}
As before, we adopt the convention that if $z \in \operatorname{Sp}(A),$ then $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H}^{-1} = 0,$ or $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H} = \infty$ \cite{Matt, Nick}.
Similarly to \eqref{eps stab B}, the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $A$ can be defined as follows \cite{Matt, Nick}:
\begin{equation} 
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(A) = \operatorname{Cl} \left( \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\| < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E) \right).
\end{equation}
% As before, this second definition tells us that the pseudospectra of $A$ describe how far perturbations in $A$ impact its spectral values. Th

As is the case in finite-dimensions, the pseudospectra of $A$ can be used to explore the conditioning of the spectrum of $A.$ When $A$ is a normal operator, $ \|(A-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z, \operatorname{Sp}(A)).$ In this case,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(A) = \operatorname{Sp}(A) + \mathfrak{B}_{\epsilon}(0),
\end{equation}
where $\mathfrak{B}_{\epsilon}(0)$ denotes the closed ball of radius $\epsilon$ centered at zero \cite{Matt}. Thus,  a perturbation of $A$ with a norm less than $\epsilon$ cannot have spectral values that are further than an $\epsilon$-distance from the spectrum of $A.$ In this sense, the spectrum of $A$ is well-conditioned. 

When $A$ is not a normal operator, its $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum may be much larger than $\operatorname{Sp}(A) + \mathfrak{B}_{\epsilon}(0).$ In this case, perturbations of $A$ with norm less than $\epsilon$  may have spectral values that are much further than an $\epsilon$-distance from the spectrum of $A,$ indicating that the spectrum of $A$ is ill-conditioned.
