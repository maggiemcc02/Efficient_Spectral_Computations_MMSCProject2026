\chapter{Background}

In this chapter, we present all necessary background material. We begin with an overview of relevant definitions and results from spectral theory. Then, we describe the common pitfalls of the discretize-and-solve approach. To see one of these pitfalls in action, we consider the famous Maxwell eigenproblem, which exhibits spurious eigenvalues when discretized with standard piecewise-linear Lagrange finite elements. 

Having explored the limitations of the discretize-and-solve approach, we introduce Matthew Colbrook's algorithm for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. When applied to the Maxwell eigenproblem, this approach avoids spurious eigenvalues, even when discretized with piecewise-linear Lagrange finite elements. However, when the discretization is coarse, Colbrook's bounds on the distance to the spectrum may be poor. In an effort to quantify the error in these bounds, and to improve these bounds without extensive uniform refinement, we explore the Dual-Weighted-Residual (DWR) method for eigenproblems and investigate its application to Colbrook's algorithm.

\subsection{The Spectra of Linear Operators}


Let $H$ be a Hilbert space with inner product $(\cdot, \cdot)_{H}$ and norm $\|\cdot \|_{H}.$
We consider a linear operator $A : D(A) \rightarrow H$ defined on a subspace $D(A) \subset H.$ We use $R(A)$ and $D(A)$ to denote the domain and range of $A,$ respectively. Furthermore, we use $N(A)$ to denote the null space, or kernel, of $A.$   

We say that $A$ is a \textit{bounded} on $H$, denoted $A \in \mathcal{B}(H),$ if there exists a constant $c>0$ such that $\|Ax\|_{H} \le c \|x\|_{H}$ for all $x \in D(A).$ When a bounded linear operator's range lies on the real line or in the complex plane, it is called a \textit{bounded linear functional} \cite{krey}. 
Furthermore, we say that $A$ is \textit{boundedly invertible} if there exists a bounded operator $B:H \rightarrow D(A)$ such that $AB=I$ and $BA \subset I,$ where $I$ denotes the identity operator on $H.$ In this case, $B$ is called the \textit{inverse} of $A$ \cite{Matt}. 


We say that $A$ is \textit{closed} if and only if it has the following property: whenever $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence in $D(A)$ such that $x_n \rightarrow x$ and $A x_n \rightarrow y,$ then $x \in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$ \cite{krey}.
We note that when $A$ is closed, being boundedly invertible is equivalent to $N(A) = \{0\}$ and $R(A) = H.$\footnote{When $N(A) =\{0\}$ and $R(A) = H,$ $A^{-1}$ is defined. Since $A$ is closed so is $A^{-1}.$ Then, the Closed Graph Theorem tells us that $A^{-1}$ is bounded \cite{krey}.} 

 To define the unique \textit{Hilbert adjoint} of $A,$ we assume that $A$ is d\textit{ensely defined}, i.e., that $D(A)$ is dense in $H.$ Furthermore, we define
\begin{equation}\label{D(A*)}
    D(A^*) = \{ w \in H : v \mapsto ( Av, w )_{H} \text{ is a bounded linear functional on D(A)}\}.
 \end{equation}
 Consider a $w \in D(A^*).$ Since $D(A)$ is dense in $H$, there exists a unique $u \in H$ such that 
 \begin{equation}
     (Av, w)_{H} = (v, w)_{H}, \qquad \forall v \in D(A).
 \end{equation}
 The Hilbert adjoint of $A$ is defined as the linear operator $A^*$ that satisfies $u = A^*w.$ When $A^* = A,$ the linear operator $A$ is said to be \textit{self-adjoint}. Furthermore, when $A$ is closed and densely defined, $A^*A$ and $AA^*$ are self adjoint. In this case, if $AA^* = A^*A,$ then $A$ is called \textit{normal}.



In this setting, the spectrum of $A,$ as stated in \eqref{SpA}, can be equivantly formulated as the following set:
\begin{equation}\label{SpA2}
    \Sp{A} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : A-zI \text{ is not boundedly invertible}\}.
\end{equation}
If $A$ is not a closed linear operator, then $\Sp{A} = \mathbb{C}.$ Thus, going forward, we will consider the case in which $A$ is a closed linear operator. In this case, $\Sp{A}$ is a closed subset of the complex plane \cite{Matt}.  

As is the case in finite dimensions, $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is an \textit{eigenvalue} of $A$ if $A-zI$ is not injective, i.e., if $N(A-zI) \ne \{0\}.$ We use $\Gamma(A)$ to denote the set of the eigenvalues of $A.$ For each $z \in \Gamma(A),$ the corresponding nullspace of $A-zI$ is the \textit{eigenspace} of $A$ corresponding to $z.$ Notice that $\Gamma(A) \subset \Sp{A}$ but that there may exist $z \in \Sp{A}\setminus \Gamma(A).$ For example, $A-zI$ may fail to be boundedly invertible because $R(A-zI)$ is not all of $H,$ even when $z$ is not an eigenvalue of $A.$

If the linear operator $A$ is closed and densely defined, the spectrum of $A$ can be related to the spectrum of its Hilbert adjoint $A^*.$ In this case, $\operatorname{Sp}(A^*) = \{\overline{z} : z \in \Sp{A}\},$ where $\overline z$ denotes the complex conjugation of $z \in \mathbb{C}.$ Moreover, if $z \notin \Sp{A}$ then $(A-zI)^{-1}$ exists and $[(A-zI)^{-1}]^* = A^* - \overline zI.$  

% With these properties in mind, every operator we will consider going forward is assumed to be linear, closed, and densely defined on a Hilbert space $H.$ 
% Furthermore, when $A$ is closed and densely defined, both $A^*A$ and $AA^*$ are self-adjoint.
% With these properties in mind, we henceforth assume that every operator A:D(A)⊂H→H under consideration is linear, closed, and densely defined.

 One of the most important tools for the analysis and computation of spectra is the resolvent operator:
\begin{equation}\label{resolvant}
    (A, z) \mapsto (A-zI)^{-1} \qquad z \notin \Sp{A}.
\end{equation}
The norm of the resolvent provides information about the distance from $z\notin \Sp{A}$ to the spectrum of $A.$ In particular,
\begin{equation}
    \| (A-zI)^{-1} \|_H^{-1} \le \operatorname{dist}(z, \Sp{A}), 
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{dist}(z, \Sp{A}) = \inf_{w \in \Sp{A}} |z - w|.
\end{equation}
If the operator $A$ is normal, we have equality:
\begin{equation}
    \| (A-zI)^{-1} \|_H^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z, \Sp{A}). 
\end{equation}
Hence, when $A$ is a normal operator, the norm of the resolvent can be used to characterize the spectrum of $A.$

To ask whether a $z\in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of the matrix $B \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n},$ we ask if $B-zI$ is invertible. However, for matrices with an ill-conditioned spectrum, a small perturbation from $B$ can change the answer from ``yes" to ``no". Thus, asking whether $B-zI$ is invertible is not a robust question. Instead, we may ask if $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|$ is large, where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the matrix norm of interest \cite{Nick}. This pattern of thinking leads to the analysis of the \textit{pseudospectra} of $B.$ In particular, for $\epsilon > 0,$ the $\epsilon$-\textit{pseudospectrum} of $B$ is the following set:
\begin{equation} \label{eps B}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(B) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}:\|(B-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} \le \epsilon \} = \operatorname{Cl} \left(\{z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(B-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} < \epsilon \} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Cl}(\cdot)$ denotes the closure of the inputted set \cite{Matt}. Here, we adopt the convention that if $z \in \operatorname{Sp}(B),$ then $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1} = 0,$ or $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\| = \infty$ \cite{Matt, Nick}. 

The $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $B$ can be equivalently defined as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eps stab B}
    \operatorname{Sp}_{\epsilon}(B) = \bigcup_{E \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}, \|E\| \le \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(B+E).
\end{equation}
This second definition tells us that the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $B$ is the region in the complex plane describing how far an $\epsilon$-sized perturbation of $B$ can move its eigenvalues \cite{Matt}. In this sense, the pseudospectra of $B$ describe the sensitivity of its eigenvalues to perturbations. The pseudospectra of $B$ can therefore be used to identify the regions of the complex plane where eigenvalue computations are unreliable and where eigenvalues alone may provide an unreliable description of the underlying system.

Recall that $B$ is a normal matrix when $B^*B=BB^*,$ where $B^*$ denotes the conjugate transpose of $B.$ When $B$ is a normal matrix, $\|(B-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{Sp}(B)).$ In this case, the $\epsilon$-pseudospectra of $B$ consist of closed $\epsilon$ balls about each eigenvalue of $B.$ Thus, an $\epsilon$-sized perturbation of $B$ cannot have eigenvalues that are further than an $\epsilon$-distance from the spectrum of $B.$ In this sense, the spectrum of $B$ is well-conditioned.  


In infinite dimensions, we consider a closed and densely defined  linear operator $A : D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H,$ defined on the Hilbert space $H.$ For each $\epsilon>0,$ the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $A$ is the following set:\footnote{Notice that in infinite dimensions, the the equality in \eqref{eps B} need not hold. This is because in infinite dimensions, the resolvent norm of an unbounded operator on a Hilbert space can be constant on open sets \cite{Matt}.}
\begin{equation}
    \Spe{A} = \operatorname{Cl}\left( \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H}^{-1} < \epsilon \}  \right).
\end{equation}
As before, we adopt the convention that if $z \in \Sp{A},$ then $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H}^{-1} = 0,$ or $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{H} = \infty$ \cite{Matt, Nick}.
Similarly to \eqref{eps stab B}, the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $A$ can be defined as follows \cite{Matt, Nick}:
\begin{equation} 
    \Spe{A} = \operatorname{Cl} \left( \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\|_H < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E) \right).
\end{equation}

As is the case in finite-dimensions, the pseudospectra of $A$ can be used to explore the conditioning of the spectrum of $A.$ When $A$ is a normal operator, $ \|(A-zI)^{-1} \|_H^{-1} = \operatorname{dist}(z, \Sp{A}).$ In this case,
\begin{equation}
    \Spe{A} = \Sp{A} + \mathfrak{B}_{\epsilon}(0),
\end{equation}
where $\mathfrak{B}_{\epsilon}(0)$ denotes the closed ball of radius $\epsilon$ centered at zero \cite{Matt}. \section{Dangerous Pitfalls of the Discretize-Then-Solve Approach}\label{pit}

The most common approach for computing the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator is the discretize-then-solve approach. This approach discretizes the infinite-dimensional operator with a finite-dimensional matrix and computes its eigenvalues. The success of this method relies on the hope that as the size of the discretization increases, the set of computed eigenvalues converges to the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator. While such an approach can be succesful for many problems, it is known to fail catastopically. 

 A common failure mode of the discretize-and-solve approach occurs when the computed spectrum contains values that do not belong to the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator. This phenomenon is referred to as spectral pollution, which is formally defined as follows:
\begin{definition}[Spectral Pollution \cite{Matt}]
    Let $\Gamma_n(A) \subset \mathbb{C}$ denote an approximation of $\Sp{A}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ We say that the sequence of approximations $\{\Gamma_n(A)\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ suffers from spectral pollution if there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \Sp{A}$ such that 
    \begin{equation}
        \liminf_{n \rightarrow \infty} \operatorname{dist}(\lambda, \Gamma_n(A))= 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}Spectral pollution is a more dangerous phenomenon than spurious eigenvalues of a fixed discretization. A spurious eigenvalue of a fixed discretization may move or disappear as the discretization is refined. However, spectral pollution can occur when there is a sequence of spurious eigenvalues that converge to a value not in the spectrum. This convergence is deceiving, as it makes the spurious limit seem like a geniune spectral value. 

Spectral invisibility occurs when the discretization of the infinite-dimensional operator causes us to miss parts of its spectrum, even as the size of the discretization increases \cite{Matt}. This failure mode is formally defined as follows:
\begin{definition}[Spectral Invisibility \cite{Matt}]
    Let $\Gamma_n(A) \subset \mathbb{C}$ denote an approximation of $\Sp{A}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ We say that the sequence of approximations $\{\Gamma_n(A)\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ suffers from spectral invisibility if there exists $\lambda \in \Sp{A}$ such that 
        \begin{equation}
        \limsup_{n \rightarrow \infty} \operatorname{dist}(\lambda, \Gamma_n(A))> 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Even when the discretize-and-solve approach avoids both spectral pollution and spectral invisibility, there is a fundamental issue that arises when an infinite-dimensional operator is discretized to quickly. For general operators, the \textit{essential spectrum} refers to the $z \in \Sp{A}$ for which $A-zI$ fails to be invertible in an `infinite-dimensional' way\footnote{We omit a formal definition of the essential spectrum because there are at least five technical definitions for non-normal operators. The curious reader can consult \cite[Ch. 8]{Matt}.} \cite{Matt}. Contrastly, the \textit{discrete spectrum} refers to the $z \in \Sp{A}$ for which $A-zI$ fails to be invertible in an `finite-dimensional' way. Thus, the discrete spectrum can be thought of the subset of $\Sp{A}$ that contains the eigenvalues of $A$ with finite multiplicity. 
Immediately discretizing $A$ with a finite matrix results in a finite, discrete set of eigenvalues. As a result, the discretize-and-solve approach may struggle to capture the essential spectrum of an operator, and to distinguish it from the discrete spectrum.  

\subsection{The Maxwell Eigenproblem and Spectral Pollution}\label{Maxwell Sect}


To illustrate the effect of these failure modes, we consider the Maxwell eigenproblem, a famous example for which na\"{i}ve finite element discretizations exhibit spectral pollution \cite{Boffi}. 

The Maxwell eigenproblem describes electromagnetic oscillations in a resonator. The standard problem is posed on a bounded Lipschitz domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3.$ The aim is to find the resonant frequencies $\omega \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ and the nonzero electromagnetic fields $(\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{H}) \in \mathbf{H}_0(\textbf{curl};\Omega) \times \mathbf{H}(\textbf{curl};\Omega)$ such that
\begin{subequations}\label{3D Max}
\begin{align}
    \textbf{curl} \, \mathbf{E} &= i \omega \mathbf{H}, \qquad &\text{in } \Omega,\label{eq:system-a}\\
    \textbf{curl} \, \mathbf{H} &= -i \omega \mathbf{E}, \qquad &\text{in } \Omega,\label{eq:system-b}\\
    \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{n} &= 0, \qquad &\text{on } \partial \Omega. \label{eq:system-c}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward facing normal, $\mathbf{H}(\textbf{curl};\Omega)$ contains the vectors in $L^2(\Omega)^3$ with $\mathbf{curl}$ in $L^2(\Omega)^3,$ and $\mathbf{H}_0(\textbf{curl};\Omega)$ contains the elements of $\mathbf{H}(\textbf{curl};\Omega)$ that have zero tangential trace on $\partial \Omega$ \cite{Matt}.

The classical formulation of the eigenvalue problem is obtained by eliminating $\mathbf{H}$ and renaming $\mathbf{E}$ as the eigenfunction $\mathbf{u}$ in \eqref{3D Max}. The resulting eigenproblem consists of finding $\omega \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ and $\mathbf{u} \ne 0$ such that
\begin{subequations}\label{3D u max}
\begin{align}
    \textbf{curl} \, \textbf{curl} \,  \mathbf{u} &= \omega^2 \mathbf{u}, \qquad &\text{in } \Omega,\\
    \text{div} \, \mathbf{u} &= 0, \qquad &\text{in }  \Omega, \\
    \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{n} &= 0, \qquad &\text{on } \partial \Omega. 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

The common variational formulation of \eqref{3D u max} seeks $\omega \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbf{H}_0(\textbf{curl}; \Omega)$ such that
\begin{equation} \label{3D var max}
    (\textbf{curl} \, \mathbf{u}, \textbf{curl} \, \mathbf{v})_{L^2(\Omega)} = \omega^2(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})_{L^2(\Omega)}, \qquad \forall \mathbf{v} \in \mathbf{H}_0(\textbf{curl}; U),
\end{equation}
where $(\cdot, \cdot)_{L^2(\Omega)}$ denotes the $L^2(\Omega)$ inner product, i.e., $(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})_{L^2(\Omega)} = \int_{\Omega} \mathbf{u} \overline{\mathbf{v}} \, d \mathbf{x}.$
Notice that the eigenfunctions of \eqref{3D var max} corresponding to nonzero values of $\omega$ are also solutions to \eqref{3D u max}. However,  at $\omega = 0,$ \eqref{3D var max} has an infinite-dimensional eigenspace containing $\textbf{grad}(\mathbf{H}_0^1(\Omega))$ \cite{Boffi}.

For simplicity, we consider the two-dimensional counterpart of \eqref{3D Max}, which shares the spectral pollution dangers of the three-dimensional problem. In particular, we set $\Omega = (0, \pi)^2$ and seek $\omega \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbf{H}(\text{rot}; \Omega)$ such that 
\begin{subequations}\label{Max}
\begin{align}
    \text{rot} \, \mathbf{E} &= i  \omega H, \qquad &\text{in } \Omega,\label{eq:system-a}\\
    \textbf{rot} \,H &= -i \omega \mathbf{E}, \qquad &\text{in } \Omega,\label{eq:system-b}\\
    \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{t} &= 0, \qquad &\text{on } \partial \Omega. \label{eq:system-c}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $\text{rot} \, \mathbf{E} = \partial_z E_2 - \partial_y E_1,$ where $\mathbf{E} = (E_1, E_2)^T,$ and $\textbf{rot} H = (\partial_y H, -\partial_x H)^T.$
Furthermore, we use $\mathbf{t}$ to denote the unit tangent of $\partial \Omega,$ $H(\textbf{rot}; \Omega)$ the denote the space of functions in $L^2(\Omega)$ with $\textbf{rot}$ in $L^2(\Omega)^2,$ and $\mathbf{H}_0(\text{rot} ; \Omega)$ to denote the space of functions in $L^2(\Omega)^2$ with $\text{rot}$ in $L^2(\Omega)$ \cite{Matt}.  

It has been shown that the nonzero eigenvalues of \eqref{Max} coincide with those of the Poisson eigenproblem with Neumann boundary conditions \cite{Boffi}. Consequently, the positive eigenvalues of \eqref{Max} are
\begin{equation}\label{exact eigs}
    \omega^2 = m^2+n^2,
    \qquad
    m,n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\},
    \qquad
    (m,n)\neq(0,0).
\end{equation}

To discretize \eqref{Max} with the finite element method, we consider the two-dimensional counterpart of \eqref{3D var max}, i.e., we seek $\omega \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbf{H}_0(\text{rot}; \Omega)$ such that
\begin{equation} \label{2D var max}
    ( \text{rot}\, \mathbf{u}, \text{rot} \, \mathbf{v})_{L^2(\Omega)} = \omega^2(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})_{L^2(\Omega)}, \qquad \forall \mathbf{v} \in \mathbf{H}_0(\text{rot}; \Omega).
\end{equation}
To transition from the infinite-dimensional setting to a finite-dimensional setting, we consider a sequence of subspaces $V_h \in \mathbf{H}_0(\text{rot}; \Omega).$ Then, we seek $\omega_h \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $\mathbf{u}_h \in V_h$ such that 
\begin{equation} \label{2D gal}
    ( \text{rot}\, \mathbf{u}_h, \text{rot} \, \mathbf{v}_h)_{L^2(\Omega)} = \omega_h^2(\mathbf{u}_h, \mathbf{v}_h)_{L^2(\Omega)}, \qquad \forall \mathbf{v}_h \in V_h.
\end{equation}
Given a basis $V_h = \operatorname{span}\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{\phi}_n\},$ \eqref{2D gal} is equivalent to the following eigenproblem:
\begin{equation} \label{Ax=b}
    A_h \, \mathbf{x} = \omega_h^2 M_h \mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the vector of coefficients for $\mathbf{u}_h,$ $A_h$ is the stiffness matrix with entries $A_{h_{ij}} = (\text{rot} \, \boldsymbol{\phi}_j, \text{rot} \, \boldsymbol{\phi}_i)_{L^2(\Omega)},$ and $M_h$ is the mass matrix with entries $M_{h_{ij}} = (\boldsymbol{\phi}_j, \boldsymbol{\phi}_i)_{L^2(\Omega)}.$ Thus, from the perspective of linear algebra, the finite elment discretization of an infinite-dimensional eigenvalue problem results in a generalized eigenproblem, where the mass matrix serves to enforce the correct inner product \cite{PatrickNote}. In the current setting, $M_h$ enforces the $L^2(\Omega)$ inner product, i.e., $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})_{L^2(\Omega)} = \mathbf{x}^* M_h \mathbf{y}$.

Edge elements get their name from the nature of their degrees of freedom, which, for the lowest-order approximation, are given by the moments along the edges of the triangulation of the mesh \cite{Boffi}. \section{Colbrook's Algorithm for Computing Pseudospectra}



The pitfalls of the discretize-then-solve approach motivate the development of alternative approaches for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. In recent work, Matthew Colbrook has proposed an alternative approach that avoids these pitfalls by working with the infinite-dimensional operator as far as possible, thereby delaying discretization. In this way, Colbrook shows that when and how discretization is introduced is crucial to the reliable computation of spectra. 

In this section, we introduce Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra. We focus on his algorithm for pseudospectral computations rather than his algorithm for spectral computations for three main reasons. First, Colbrook's algorithm for pseudospectra can be applied to a much broader class of operators than his algorithm for spectra. Second, Colbrook's algorithm for pseudospectra forms the main computational component of his algorithm for computing spectra. Finally, as discussed in Section \ref{pseudo sec}, the pseudospectra of an operator are often more informative than the spectrum alone. 

In the first subsection, we introduce the fundamental tool for Colbrook's algorithm, namely, the injection modulus of an operator. In the second subsection, we use this tool to build Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra. Then, in the third subsection, we specialize the algorithm to the finite element setting. Finally, in the final subsection, we demonstrate the success of Colbrook's algorithm by applying it to the Maxwell eigenproblem. 


\subsection{Characterizing the Spectrum with Injection Moduli}
Let $A$ be a closed and densely defined linear operator on $H.$ The first ingredient for Colbrook's algorithm is the \textit{injection modulus} of $A,$ which is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\inf}(A) = \{ \|Ax\|_H : x \in D(A), \|x\|_H = 1\}.
\end{equation}
The injection modulus is a quantitative measure of `how injective' an operator is \cite{Matt}. In this way, it can be viewed as a generalization of the smallest singular value of a finite-dimensional matrix. However, we must be careful not to confuse the injection modulus with the smallest singular value, since infinite-dimensional operators can exhibit behaviours that are quite different from those of finite-dimensional operators \cite{Matt}. For example, an infinite-dimensional operator $A$ can be injective but have $\sigma_{\inf}(A)=0.$ This may happen when there is a sequence of unit vectors, $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, such that $\|A x_n \|_H \rightarrow 0$ but there is no nonzero $x \in D(A)$ that satisfies $\|Ax\|_H=0.$ 

One of the most fundamental tools in Colbrook's algorithm is the following function of $z 
\in \mathbb{C},$
\begin{equation}
    \gamma(z, A) = \min \left\{ \sigma_{\inf}(A-zI), \sigma_{\inf}(A^* - \overline{z}I) \right\}.
\end{equation}
With this, Colbrook characterizes the resolvent norm $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_H$ in the following way:
\begin{lemma}[Injection Moduli and Resolvent Norms \cite{Matt}]
   Let $A$ be a closed and densely defined operator on a Hilbert space $H.$ We have that
   \begin{equation} \label{gamma}
       \gamma(z, A) = \|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_H^{-1},
   \end{equation}
   where we adopt the convention that $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_H^{-1} = 0$ if $z \in \Sp{A}.$ Furthermore, if $z \notin \Sp{A},$ then $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I).$
\end{lemma} \label{gamma and norm}

In finite dimensions, we have that $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I)$ for every $z \in \mathbb{C}.$ However, in infinite dimensions, it is possible to have $z \in \Sp{A}$ and $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) \ne \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I).$ To see this, consider the right-shift operator as defined in \eqref{right shift}, i.e., the operator $A:l^2(\mathbb{N}) \rightarrow l^2(\mathbb{N)}$ defined by $A(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots).$ This operator satisfies $\|Ax\|_{l^2(\mathbb{N})} = \|x\|_{l^2(\mathbb{N})}.$ Thus, $\sigma_{\inf}(A) = 1.$ Now consider its Hilbert adjoint $A^*,$ which is the left-shift operator $A^*(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots).$ Notice that $A^*(1, 0, \dots) = (0, 0, \dots).$ Hence, $\sigma_{\inf}(A) \ne \sigma_{\inf}(A^*) = 0.$

With Lemma \ref{gamma and norm} in mind, we can define the spectrum of $A$ as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{gamma Sp}
    \Sp{A} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) = 0\}.
\end{equation}
Similarly, we can define the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum as the following set:
\begin{equation} \label{gamma eps Sp}
    \Spe{A} = \operatorname{Cl} \left(\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) < \epsilon\}\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, to approximate $\Sp{A}$ and $\Spe{A},$ we can approximate $\gamma(z, A).$ This idea underpins Colbrook's algorithms for computing the spectra and pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators. As discussed, we focus on Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra because it applies to a broad class of operators, it forms the main computational component of his algorithm for computing spectra, and because pseudospectral computations tend to be much more interesting than spectral computations. 


\subsection{Computing Pseudospectra by Operator Folding}

Colbrook's algorithm for computing the pseudospectra of an operator $A$ relies on the characterization of $\Spe{A}$ using $\gamma(z,A)$ in \eqref{gamma eps Sp}. The key idea is to approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ at chosen values of $z \in \mathbb{C}$ and to retain the values of $z$ for which $\gamma(z, A) < \epsilon.$ To construct this methodology, Colbrook makes the following assumption:
\begin{assumption}[Required Convergence of $\Phi_n(z, A)$ \cite{Matt}]\label{assump}
    To compute $\Spe{A},$ assume that we can compute functions $\Phi_n(z, A)$ such that 
    \begin{equation}
        \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \sup_{z \in \mathcal{C}} | \Phi_n(z, A) - \gamma(z, A) | = 0 \qquad \forall \text{ compact } \mathcal{C}\in \mathbb{C}, 
    \end{equation}
    where the speed of convergence is allows to rely on $A$ and $\mathcal{C}.$ Furthermore, we assume that $\gamma(z, A) \le \Phi_n(z, A)$ for sufficiently large $n$ (depending on $A$ but not on $z$).
\end{assumption}

To satisfy this assumption, we introduce \textit{operator folding}. Suppose that $A$ is a closed and densely defined operator on a separable Hilbert space $H,$ i.e., a Hilbert space that contains a countable orthonormal basis \cite{Matt}. Let $\{\mathcal{P}_n\}$ be orthogonal projections such that $\mathcal{P}_n^*\mathcal{P}_n$ strongly converges to the identity as $n \rightarrow \infty$ \cite{Matt}. The key idea behind operator folding is to approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ using the following relation:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\inf}((A-zI)\mathcal{P}_n^*) =  \sqrt{\sigma_{\inf}(\mathcal{P}_n(A-zI)^*(A-zI)\mathcal{P}_n^*)}.
\end{equation}
That is, we approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ with 
\begin{equation} \label{phi Pn}
    \Phi_n(z, A) = \min \left\{\sigma_{\inf}((A-zI) \mathcal{P}_n^*), \sigma_{\inf}((A-zI)^*\mathcal{P}_n^*) \right\},
\end{equation}
which satisfies Assumption \ref{assump} under suitable conditions of $D(A)$ and $D(A^*)$ \cite{Matt, kato}.

\section{The Dual-Weighted Residual (DWR) Method for Symmetric Eigenproblems}

In this section, we provide an overview of the Dual-weighted Residual (DWR) method that we apply to the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm. In the first subsection, we discuss the role of \textit{a posteriori} error estimates in constructing adaptive finite element methods. \subsection{Adaptive Finite Element Methods}

As we have seen so far, the finite element methodology provides an approximation to the solution of an underlying infinite-dimensional variational problem. As a result, we may wonder how the error of this approximation can be measured, controlled, and effectively minimized \cite{Oden}. To resolve these questions, we turn to \textit{a posteriori} error estimates. Given a computed approximation, \textit{a posteriori} error estimates provide an estimate of the error in that particular approximation \cite{Scott}. As a result, \textit{A posteriori} error estimates are often used to determine which parts of the current discretization should be modified, in an attempt to reduce the error in the current approximation \cite{Scott}. 

This idea gives rise to adaptive finite-element methods (AFEMs), which are techniques for choosing a finite-dimensional space, $V_h,$ that is optimal with respect to computational complexity and accuracy \cite{Scott}. This is typically done by adaptively refining the mesh used to construct $V_h$ in regions where the error estimate is large, giving rise to adaptive mesh refinement techniques (AMRs) \cite{Scott}. 
These adaptive mesh refinement techniques are particularly useful when the error estimate is large only in certain regions of the domain. In this case, choosing a fine uniform mesh may be a waste of computational effort. In contrast, adaptive mesh techniques refine the mesh only in regions where the estimated error is large. For this reason, AFEMs, that take the form of AMRs, are useful tools for improving the efficiency and accuracy of finite element approximations.

When applying the finite element methodology to solve a problem arising in application, scientists and engineers are often interested in a derived quantity of interest, rather than in the solution field over the whole domain \cite{joe}. For example, when solving a problem in fluid dynamics, we may be interested in the vorticity of an eddy, rather than the velocity field \cite{Scott}. In the case of Colbrook's algorithm, we are interested in the error in the computed eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems, rather than in the computed eigenfunctions. 

Following this line of thinking, it may be more beneficial to compute and control error estimates in a prescribed quantity of interest rather than in the solution field itself. Classical \textit{a posteriori} error analysis consists of estimating the error of the solution field in the $L^2$ or energy norms \cite{Becker}. In contrast, the Dual-Weighted Residual (DWR) method provides error estimates for prescribed quantities of interest. Thus, to efficiently resolve the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm, we use the DWR method to drive adaptive mesh refinement based on the estimated error in the computed eigenvalues.

\subsection{The DWR Method for the Eigenvalue Error}

% The DWR method was established by Rolf Rannacher and Ronald Becker, amoungst others, in the 1900s and early 2000s. A comprehensive review of its development can be found in \cite{Becker}. 

To illustrate the ideas underlying the DWR method, we begin in an abstract setting. 
Let $u$ denote the solution of the underlying infinite-dimensional problem, often referred to as the \textit{primal problem}. Let $J(\cdot)$ be a scalar function used to compute the quantity of interest $J(u).$ When we discretize the primal problem with the finite element method, we obtain a solution approximation, $u_h,$ and the corresponding approximation of the quantity of interest, $J(u_h).$ The DWR method aims to both estimate and control the error in the computed quantity of interest,
\begin{equation}
    |J(u) - J(u_h)|.
\end{equation}

 
To estimate this error, the DWR method embeds the given problem into the framework of optimal control \cite{Becker}. To do so, the method introduces an associated \textit{dual problem} that depends on the chosen quantity of interest. Using the solution of the dual problem, the method provides an error estimate in the form of a primal residual weighted by a `sensitivity parameter' obtained from the dual solution. In this way, the dual solution is used to describe sensitivity of the quantity of interest to errors in the computed primal solution, thereby allowing for mesh adaptation driven by the error in the computed quantity of interest rather than by the error in the computed primal solution \cite{Becker}.

where $M$ is assumed to be self-adjoint and positive semidefinite  \cite{Bangerth}.

As per usual, we prefer the variational formulation

    a(u, v) = \lambda m(u, v) \qquad \forall v \in V,
\end{equation}
% where

where $a(\cdot, \cdot)$ and $m(\cdot, \cdot)$ are the sesquilinear forms induced by $A$ and $M,$ respectively.
Furthermore, all eigenfunctions that satisfy \eqref{var eig} are assumed to be normalized by $m(u, u) = 1.$

To discretize this eigenproblem, we introduce the finite-dimensional subspace $V_h \subset V$ and seek $\lambda_h \in \mathbb{C}$ and nonzero $u_h \in V_h$ that satisfy
\begin{equation} \label{gal eig}
    a(u_h, v_h) = \lambda_h m(u_h, v_h) \qquad \forall v_h \in V_h,
\end{equation}
where it is assumed that $m(u_h, u_h) = 1.$
With this, our goal is to control the error in $\lambda_h$. In other words, the quantity of interest is $J(u_h) = \lambda_h.$


As previously discussed, the DWR method introduces a dual problem whose solution is used to describe the sensitivity of the quantity of interest to errors in the primal solution. For a general eigenvalue problem, the dual problem takes the form of the adjoint eigenproblem:
\begin{equation} \label{dual eig}
    A^* u^* = \lambda^*Mu^*, 
\end{equation}
where $A^*$ denotes the Hilbert adjoint of $A$. In the present setting, however, the auxiliary eigenproblems of Colbrook's algorithm are symmetric. Hence, the primal and dual eigenproblems coincide. This leads to the following symmetric version of the DWR error estimate, which is stated and proved in \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}:
\begin{corollary}[DWR Method for a Symmetric Eigenproblem \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}]
    Consider an eigenpair $\{u_h, \lambda_h\} \in V_h \times \mathbb{C}$ that solves \eqref{gal eig}. 
    Define the primal eigenvalue residual as
    \begin{equation} \label{resid}
        \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(\cdot) \defeq a(u_h, \cdot) - \lambda_h m(u_h, \cdot).
    \end{equation}
    We have the error representation
    \begin{equation} \label{DWR}
        (\lambda - \lambda_h)(1 - \xi_h) = \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h),
    \end{equation}
    for an arbitrary $\varphi_h \in V_h,$ with the remainder term
    \begin{equation}
        \xi_h = \frac{1}{2} m(u-u_h, u-u_h).
    \end{equation}

\end{corollary}

% \footnote{In \cite{Bangerth} and \cite{Heuveline}, the general result for non-symmetric eigenproblems is formulated in the Hilbert-space setting, where $A$ and $M$ map from $V$ to $V,$ as opposed to the current antidual setting, in which $A$ and $M$ map from $V$ to its antidual $V^{\times}$. Fortunately, the result and its proof are expressed in terms of the associated variational forms of the eigenproblems, which take the same form in the Hilbert-space and antidual settings. Hence, the result carries over to the current setting unchanged.}:

With this result, we can estimate the error in approximating an eigenvalue of \eqref{eig prob} with an eigenvalue of \eqref{gal eig}. Furthermore, we can localize the global error estimate \eqref{DWR} to obtain local error contributions for each cell of the mesh that underlies the current discretization. With this in mind, the localization of \eqref{DWR} can be used to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique. 




\subsection{Localization of Error Estimates}

% To formulate an adaptive refinement technique, we require local error estimates that describe the contribution that each cell of the current mesh makes to the error in the quantity of interest. These local error estimates are obtained via a localization procedure applied to \textit{a posteriori} error estimates, such as \eqref{DWR}.

A common approach for localizing DWR error estimates is to decompose the integrals appearing in the error estimate over the cells of the mesh and to integrate by parts on each cell. This results in a sum of residual contributions from both the cells and the facets of the mesh. Since interior cell facets are shared by two cells, it is common practice to equally distribute the interior facet contributions between adjacent cells. In doing so, the original error estimate is decomposed into the sum of error contributions for each cell of the mesh. For worked examples of this localization procedure, the curious reader may consult \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline, logg, joe}. 

Traditionally, this localization procedure had to be performed by hand on a problem-by-problem basis. Consequently, the local error indicators required for adaptive mesh refinement had to be re-derived and implemented for each new problem of interest. As a result, the practical use of DWR error control was limited to expert users \cite{logg}. This difficulty was addressed by Marie Rognes and Anders Logg, who devised a method for automating the localization of DWR error estimates \cite{logg}. 


Let $\mathcal{T}_h$ denote the mesh underlying the current discretization, let $T$ index each cell of the mesh, and let $\partial T$ denote the boundary of $T.$ In \cite{logg}, Rognes and Logg noticed that for a large class of problems, the localization procedure described above takes a specific form. In particular, they noticed that localization results in an error estimate that can be written as a sum of contributions from the cells and the facets of the mesh. In the present setting, this observation yields the following localization of \eqref{DWR}:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \{ (R(u_h, \lambda_h), u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r(u_h, \lambda_h), u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)}\},
\end{equation}
where $R(u_h, \lambda_h)$ and $r(u_h, \lambda_h)$ denote the cell residual and facet residual, respectively \cite{joe}. Here, $(\cdot, \cdot)_{L^2(T)}$ and $(\cdot, \cdot)_{L^2(\partial T)}$ denote the $L^2$ inner product, where the integration is performed over $T$ and $\partial T,$ respectively. 

With this observation, Rognes and Logg show that by solving local problems on each cell $T$ of the mesh, we can compute piecewise polynomial approximations $R_T$ and $r_{\partial T}$ to $R(u_h, \lambda_h) |_T$ and $r(u_h, \lambda_h)|_{\partial T},$ respectively \cite{logg}. In doing so, the primal residual can approximated via
\begin{equation}
    \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h)  \approx  \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \{ (R_T, u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r_{\partial T}, u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)}\},
\end{equation}
which yields the approximate local error contributions
\begin{equation} \label{local eta}
    \eta_T \defeq |  (R_T, u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r_{\partial T}, u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)} |.
\end{equation}
These local error contributions can be used to indicate the regions of the mesh that contribute the most to the error in the desired quanitty of interest. As a result, they can be used to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique based on the error in the desired quantity of interest. 

Rognes and Logg's automated localization procedure was implemented in Firedrake as a part of Joseph Flood's Master's Thesis \cite{joe}. This implementation has since been further developed by Patrick Farrell and Pablo Brubeck \cite{pablo}. For this project, we build upon the existing implementation and adapt it to the auxiliary eigenproblems arising in Colbrook's algorithm. We therefore omit the details of the local problems used to obtain \eqref{local eta} and refer the reader to \cite{joe, logg, pablo} for further details on the localization procedure and its implementation.

Notice that by applying Galerkin orthogonality, the primal residual in \eqref{resid} can be written as $\rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u-\varphi_h) = \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u)$ for any $\varphi_h \in V_h.$ Thus, to compute the global error estimate \eqref{DWR}, the subtraction could be suppressed \cite{Bangerth}. However, after localization, we do not expect Galerkin orthogonality to hold on each cell of the mesh. Thus, we expect the choice of $\varphi_h$ to impact the local error estimates \eqref{local eta} and the adaptive refinement procedure that results.

Two common choices for $\varphi_h$ are $\varphi_h = u_h$ and $\varphi_h = I_h u,$ where $I_hu$ denotes the interpolation of $u$ onto $V_h.$ These choices give the weights $u - u_h$ and $u - I_h u$ in \eqref{DWR} and \eqref{local eta}, respectively. The first represents the discretization error in the eigenfunction while the second represents the component of $u$ that is not represented by its interpolant in $V_h.$ In \cite{balance_errs}, it was shown that choosing $\varphi_h = u_h$ allows for the separation of the error incurred by discretization and the error incurred from inexactly solving \eqref{gal eig}. As a result, we adopt the first choice and take $u- \varphi_h = u - u_h$ in our error estimates\footnote{We note that both choices were tested numerically and produced similar results.}.

To compute either $u-u_h$ or $u - I_h u_h$, we need the eigenfunction $u$ of the infinite-dimensional problem \eqref{eig prob}. In practice, we do not have access to $u.$ If we did, there would be no need to approximate it. Thus, for the practical computation of \eqref{DWR} and $\eqref{local eta},$ we have to approximate the terms involving $u.$ In particular, we have to apprroximate $e \defeq u-u_h$ in the remainder term of \eqref{DWR}, as well as $e_h \defeq u - \varphi_h$ in \eqref{DWR} and \eqref{local eta}. 

There are many strategies for approximating $e$ and $e_h$, each of which is discussed in \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}. The most common approaches approximate $u$ in an `enriched' finite element space, $V_h^+,$  that is obtained by increasing the degree of the piecewise polynomial basis used to construct $V_h.$ To obtain an `enriched' approximation $u_h^+$ to $u$ in $V_h^+,$ there are two common approaches. We discuss both in turn:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global Higher-Order Approximation.}
    The simplest approach is to solve for $u_h^+$ by solving \eqref{gal eig} with the choice of $V_h^+$ instead of $V_h.$ An advantage of this approach is that it is easy to implement in Firedrake, which takes care of the finite element assembly, solvers, and interpolation required for $\varphi_h = I_h u_h^+.$ A disadvantage is the cost of resolving \eqref{gal eig} in a more-expensive, higher-degree, space. 
    \item \textbf{Local Higher-Order Approximation.}
    An alternative approach is to use patch-wise extrapolation to approximate $u_h^+$ using $u_h$ without having to re-solve \eqref{gal eig}. Rognes and Logg describe a least-squares implementation of this approach in \cite{logg}. 
\end{enumerate}
Following Flood, Farrell, and Brubeck, we choose the first of the two approaches because of its straightforward implementation in Firedrake \cite{joe, pablo}. The patch-wise extrapolation required for the second choice is not yet readily available in Firedrake and could thus be a topic of future work.

\section{The Inner Loop: An Adaptive Refinement Technique for Colbrook's Algorithm}

As discussed, Colbrook's algorithm can be used to compute bounds on the distance to the spectrum without suffering from the pitfalls of the discretize-then-solve approach. To obtain these bounds, we compute the smallest eigenvalues of auxiliary eigenproblems for the two folded operators $(A-zI)^*(A-zI)$ and $(A-zI)(A-zI)^*.$ However, as shown in Section \ref{Matt Max}, if the discretization of these eigenproblems is too coarse, the computed bounds from Colbrook's algorithm may be poor. 

In an effort to improve these bounds, we apply the DWR method to estimate the error in the computed smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems. We use these error estimates in two ways. First, we use the error estimate to correct the computed eigenvalues, thereby improving the computed eigenvalues, and thus the computed bounds on the distance to the spectrum. Second, we use localized DWR error estimates to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique that concentrates the computational effort in regions of the mesh where the computed eigenvalue error is large. 

In this section, we explore the details of our application of DWR to Colbrook's algorithm. In the first subsection, we explore the needed assumptions for the succesful application of the DWR error estimate in \eqref{DWR} to the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm. In the second subsection, we discuss our adaptation of \eqref{DWR} to account for eigenvalues of multiplicity greater than one. Finally, in the last three subsections, we outline our methodology for pairing DWR with Colbrook's algorithm and demonstrate its success when applied to the Maxwell eigenproblem. 


\subsection{The Needed Assumptions}\label{ASSUMPSect}

The DWR error estimate in \eqref{DWR} is formulated for infinite-dimensional eigenproblems as opposed to infinite-dimensional spectral computations. In particular, \eqref{DWR} is derived under the assumption that the desired eigenvalue, $\lambda,$ has an associated eigenfunction $u.$ As a result, the error estimate requires an approximation of the eigenvalue, $\lambda_h,$ and an approximation of the corresponding discrete eigenfunction, $u_h$. 

In Algorithm \ref{pseudospec}, the approximated bounds on the distance to the spectrum, $\Phi_n(z, A),$ are obtained by computing the bottom of the spectrum of the two folded operators $(A-zI)^*(A-zI)$ and $(A-zI)(A-zI)^*.$ There is no requirement that these spectral bottoms are eigenvalues of finite multiplicity with associated eigenfunctions. For example, there can be values of $z$ in the essential spectrum of an operator $A$ for which $\|(A-zI)x_n\| \rightarrow 0,$ $\|x_n\|_{H} = 1,$ and $\{x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb{N}} \in D(A)$ converges weakly to zero\footnote{This phenomenon is called a Weyl sequence and can be used to characterize the essential spectrum of an operator. For more information, see Chapter 8 of \cite{Matt}.} \cite{Matt}. 

With this in mind, it is not clear how to apply the DWR error estimate for eigenvalues when the computed eigenfunction $u_h$ does not reflect a geniune eigenfunction $u.$ We therefore restrict our attention to operators for which the bottom of the spectrum of each corresponding folded operator is an eigenvalue of finite multiplicity. The precise conditions on the operator $A$ for which this is the case is beyond the scope of this current report, and are left as a task for future work. Furthermore, in future work, we aim to extend the current DWR framework to acount for folded operators whose spectral bottom is an element of the essential spectrum.

\subsection{The DWR Method for Multiple Eigenvalues}


Assuming that the smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm approximate finite-multiplicity eigenvalues of the corresponding infinite-dimensional folded operators with finite multiplicity, we still need to account for the possibility that the target eigenvalue has multiplicity greater than one.

To see this, consider the case in which the closed and densely defined linear operator $A:D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H$ is self-adjoint. It is known that self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues. Thus, we restrict our spectral search to $z \in \mathbb{R}.$ The folded operator for a given $z \in \mathbb{R}$ is  $(A-zI)^*(A-zI) = (A-zI)(A-zI)^* = (A-zI)^2.$ Thus, if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ with multiplicity greater than one, then $(\lambda - z)^2$ is an eigenvalue of the folded operator with multiplicity that is at least the multiplicity of $\lambda.$ 

% For example, consider the Maxwell eigenproblem \eqref{Max} and $z\in \mathbb{R}.$ Furthermore, consider the eigenvalue $\omega^2 = 1$ with multiplicity two. For all values of $z\in \mathbb{R}$ that are closer to $\omega^2 = 1$ than to any other value of $\omega^2,$ the smallest eigenvalue of the corresponding folded operator is $(1-z)^2$ with multiplicity two.


Let $\lambda$ be the smallest eigenvalue of an infinite-dimensional eigenproblem. If $\lambda$ has a multiplicity $m > 1,$ then a finite element discretization yields $m$ eigenvalues, $\lambda_{h, i}$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, m,$ that approximate $\lambda,$ each with a corresponding discrete eigenfunction, $u_{h, i}$ \cite{Boffi, slep}. Since the eigenspace associated with $\lambda$ is $m$-dimensional, each $u_{h, i}$ may approximate any linear combination of eigenfunctions that belong to the target eigenspace. 


To use DWR to estimate the error in the smallest computed eigenvalue, we require an enriched approximation $u_h^+$ that corresponds to the chosen discrete eigenfunction $u_h.$ When $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue with multiplicity one, this matching is simple: we compute $u_h$ and $u_h^+,$ normalize them, and align their phases. When $\lambda$ has multiplicity $m>1$, the primal and enriched solves may approximate different bases for the same $m$-dimensional eigenspace. As a result, it is more challenging to choose a $u_h^+$ that behaves as an enriched approximation of the chosen $u_h.$ 

To overcome this challenge, we propose a least squares approach to matching $u_h$ with an enriched approximation $u_h^+.$ In particular, given the enriched approximation of the $m$-dimensional eigenbasis, we construct the linear combination of computed enriched eigenfunctions that `best matches' $u_h.$ Here, the notion of a `best match' is decided using a least squares problem. Given the computed enriched basis $ E_h^+ \defeq \{u_{h, 1}^+, u_{h, 2}^+, \dots, u_{h, m}^+\},$ we take $u_h^+$ to be the solution of
\begin{equation}\label{least}
    \arg \min_{v \in E_h^+} \|u_h - v \|_H,
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_H$ is the norm associated with the underlying Hilbert space $H.$
% \begin{equation} \label{least}
%      \arg \min_{(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_m) \in \mathbb{C}^m}\left \| u_h - \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i u_{h, i}^+ \right \|_H
% \end{equation}
% and set $u_h^+ = $


To solve this problem, we formulate it as a matrix problem. For $u_h^+$ to solve \eqref{least}, the residual $r^+ \defeq u_h - u_h^+$ must be orthogonal to every direction in $E_h^+$. This follows from the fact that if $u_h^+$ is the `best match,' then moving in any direction in $E_h^+$ cannot decrease the residual. Thus, we require $(r^+, u_{h, i}^+)_H = 0$ for $i=1, 2, \dots, m.$ Substituting in $r^+ = u_h - \sum_{j=1}^{m} \alpha_ju_{h, j}^+,$ we obtain the $m$-dimensional matrix problem
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{m} \alpha_j (u_{h, j}^+, u_{h, i}^+)_H = (u_h, u_{h, i}^+)_H, \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots, m,
\end{equation}
which we solve for the coefficients $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_m,$ that define our `best match' $u_h^+.$


In solving this matrix problem, we obtain an enriched eignfunction $u_h^+$ that `best' represents the chosen primal eigenfunction $u_h$ within the computed enriched eigenbasis. This allows us to apply the DWR error estimate \eqref{DWR} to eigenvalues of multiplicity greater than one.
As a result, we can use \eqref{DWR} to estimate the error in the smallest eigenvalues that arise in Colbrook's algorithm. The details of this implementation are discussed in the next subsection.

Let $j \in \{1, 2\}$ index the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem for a set $z\in \operatorname{Grid}$ in Algorithm \ref{pseudospec}. For each $j,$ the four steps of our adaptive mesh refinement technique take the following form:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*}.] 
    \item \textbf{Solve:} For a fixed $z \in \operatorname{Grid}$ in Algorithm \ref{pseudospec},
    solve the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem on its current finite element space $V_h^{(j)}.$ Retain the smallest eigenvalue $\mu_n^{(i)}(z)$ and the corresponding discrete eigenfunction $u_h^{(j)}.$ Construct the enriched space $V_h^{(j), +}$ by increasing the polynomial degree that defines $V_h^{(j)}.$ Solve the $j$-th auxilliary eigenproblem in the enriched space.
    
    \item \textbf{Estimate:} Solve the least-squares problem \eqref{least} to compute the enriched eigenfunction that best matches $u_h^{(j)}.$ Apply the DWR error estimate \eqref{DWR} to obtain the following global error estimate:
    \begin{equation}
        \eta^{(j)}(z) \approx  \mu^{(j)}(z) - \mu_n^{(j)}(z).
    \end{equation}
    Using this error estimate, compute the corrected eigenvalue, $\mu_{n,c}^{(j)}(z),$  as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        \mu_{n,c}^{(j)}(z) \defeq \eta^{(j)}(z) + \mu_n^{(j)}(z).
    \end{equation}
    Use the method of Rognes and Logg to compute the local error indicators $\eta_T^{(j)}(z)$, as given by \eqref{local eta}, for each cell $T$ in the mesh that defines $V_h^{(j)}$ \cite{logg, joe, pablo}. 

    
    \item \textbf{Mark:}\footnote{This marking scheme was obtained from \cite{Pslides} where it is said to be simpler than the famous Dörfler marking routine, but performs similarly in practice \cite{Dorf, Pslides}.} 
    Mark the cells that have a local error that is considered too large. For our implementation, we mark the cells whose local error estimate satisfies
    \begin{equation} \label{mark}
        \eta_T^{(j)}(z) \ge \theta \max_{k} \eta_k^{(j)}(z), \, \text{ for some } \, \theta \in [0,1].
    \end{equation}
    Unless otherwise stated, we take $\theta = 1/2.$

    
    \item \textbf{Refine:} Refine the marked cells, thereby defining a new finite element function space for the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem. To refine our marked cells, we employ Netgen's refinement technique, as implemented by Flood, Farrell, and Brubeck \cite{NGSolve, Schoberl1997, pablo}.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Application to the Maxwell Eigenproblem}\label{DWRMatt}

We now return to the Maxwell eigenproblem stated in \eqref{Max}. In Section \ref{Matt Max}, we applied Colbrook's algorithm to the Maxwell operator \eqref{max op}. The numerical experiments in that section 
showed that Colbrook's approach avoids the spectral pollution exhibited by the discretize-then-solve approach of Section \ref{Maxwell Sect}. However, as shown in Figure \ref{FirstMatt}, coarse discretizations of the auxiliary eigenproblems can produce poor bounds on the distance to the spectrum. In an effort to compute these bounds accurately, without resorting to extensive uniform refinement, we now couple Colbrook's algorithm with the adaptive mesh refinement technique in Section \ref{AMR}.

To do so, we take the starting mesh to be an unstructured triangular mesh generated by Netgen, with the maximum element diameter of $\pi/32$ \cite{Schoberl1997}. We take $V_h$ to be the space of linear Lagrange elements satisfying the zero tangential trace boundary condition in \eqref{Max}. The enriched space $V_h^+$ is obtained by increasing the polynomial degree of $V_h$ by one. As in Section \ref{Matt Max}, we take $\operatorname{Grid} \subset \mathbb{R}.$ In particular, we set
\begin{equation} \label{2grid}
    \operatorname{Grid} = \{2 + jh : j = 0, \dots, 100\}.
\end{equation}
Notice that, as opposed to the choice of $\operatorname{Grid}$ in Section \ref{Matt Max}, i.e., \eqref{1grid}, we no longer consider test points near $z=0.$ Recall from Section \ref{ASSUMPSect} that our adaptive mesh refinement technique can only be applied when the bottom of the spectrum of the folded operator is an eigenvalue of finite multiplicity. For the Maxwell operator \eqref{max op}, the choice of $\operatorname{Grid}$ in \eqref{2grid} avoids the case in which this smallest eigenvalue has infinite multiplicity. Further details on this choice are given in Appendix \ref{AppendMax}.

% As discussed in Section \ref{Maxwell Sect}, the Maxwell problem \eqref{2D var max} has zero eigenvalue with an infinite-dimensional eigenspace. However, to apply the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR}, we require the smallest eigenvalue of the folded operator to have a finite multiplicity. We therefore restrict our test points to a portion of the real line that avoids the infinite-multiplicity eigenvalue of the folded operator. To couple Colbrook's algorithm with the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR}, we iterate over each $z \in \operatorname{Grid}$ and compute the smallest eigenvalues, $\mu_n(z)$ and $\mu_n^+(z),$ of the auxiliary eigenproblem, \eqref{max aux}, posed on $V_h$ and $V_h^+,$ respectively. We then solve the least squares problem \eqref{least} to match the computed eigenfunction $u_h \in V_h$ with an enriched eigenfunction $u_h^+ \in V_h^+.$ Using $u_h$ and $u_h^+$, we compute the DWR error estimate $\eta(z) \approx \mu(z) - \mu_n(z),$ where $\mu(z)$ denotes the target eigenvalue. Using this error estimate, we compute the corrected eigenvalue $\mu_{n, c}(z) \defeq \eta(z) + \mu_n(z).$ Furthermore, we compute the corrected bound on the distance to the spectrum $\Phi_{n,c}(z,A) = \sqrt{ \mu_{n,c}(z) }.$ 
Using the localization procedure of Rognes and Logg, we compute a local error estimate $\eta_T(z)$ for each cell $T$ of the mesh that defines $V_h$ and $V_h^+$. We then mark and refine all cells for which \eqref{mark} holds. This results in a new mesh and, consequently, a new $V_h$ and $V_h^+$ for the discretization of the auxiliary eigenproblem. 

This process is repeated for each $z \in \operatorname{Grid}$ until a user-defined stopping criterion has been satisfied. Notice that the DWR-corrected bound is substantially more accurate than the bound obtained on a uniform mesh. This result further motivates the coupling of Colbrook's algorithm with the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \eqref{AMR}.

\section{The Outer Loop: Pairing Inner and Outer Adaptivity for Colbrook's Algorithm}

With the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR} at hand, we can efficiently solve the auxiliary eigenproblems that arise in Colbrook's algorithm. However, two questions remain. First, how should we pick the test points in the complex plane, that is, the set $\operatorname{Grid} \subset \mathbb{C},$ so that the computational effort of Colbrook's algorithm is concentrated near the desired $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum? Second, for a given $z \in \operatorname{Grid},$ when should we adaptively refine the auxiliary eigenproblems? For example, if $z$ is far from the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum, there is little benefit in considering nearby points in the complex plane. Furthermore, for a far-away $z,$ there is little benefit in computing an accurate bound on the distance to the spectrum.

% Finally, if we decide to apply the refinement technique to an auxiliary eigenproblem, what stopping criterion should we enforce? 

In this section, we answer these two questions. Our answer to the first question will determine a method for adaptively picking test points in the complex plane. Our answer to the second question will determine the relationship between `outer refinement' of the complex plane and `inner refinement' of the auxiliary eigenproblems that arise at each test point. Together, these ideas give rise to our proposed methodology, which combines both inner and outer refinement with Colbrook's algorithm to efficiently resolve the pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators. 


\subsection{The Inner Refinement Criteria}

Our adaptive mesh refinement technique in Section \ref{AMR} estimates the error in the computed smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems arising in Colbrook's algorithm. In particular, for each auxiliary eigenproblem we compute 
\begin{equation} \label{eta ests}
    \eta^{(1)}(z) \approx  \mu^{(1)}(z) - \mu_n^{(1)}(z), \text{ and } \eta^{(2)}(z) \approx  \mu^{(2)}(z) - \mu_n^{(2)}(z),
\end{equation}
respectively. Moreover, in our adaptive mesh refinement technique, we use \eqref{eta ests} to correct our computed eigenvalues as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{corrected}
    \mu_{n,c} ^{(1)}(z) \defeq  \eta^{(1)}(z) + \mu_n^{(1)}(z), \text{ and } \mu_{n,c} ^{(2)}(z) \defeq  \eta^{(2)}(z) + \mu_n^{(2)}(z).
\end{equation}

This technique focuses on error estimates for the computed eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems. However, the quantity of interest for Colbrook's algorithm is the approximate bound on the distance to the spectrum, i.e., $\Phi_n(z, A)$ in \eqref{approx phi}. As a result, we want to use \eqref{eta ests} to approximate the error in $\Phi_n(z, A),$ that is,
\begin{equation} \label{phi err}
    | \gamma(z, A) - \phi_n(z, A)| = \left| \sqrt{ \min { \mu^{(1)}(z), \mu^{(1)}(z) \}} } - \sqrt{ \min \{ \mu_n^{(1)}(z), \mu_n^{(1)}(z) \}} \right|.
\end{equation}
Furthermore, we want to use the error in $\Phi_n(z, A)$ to drive the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR}.

To use \eqref{eta ests} to construct an error bound for \eqref{phi err}, we first note that for scalars $a. b, c \in \mathbb{R},$ $|\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}| \le c$ implies that $|a - b| \le \sqrt{c}.$ Thus, to construct an error bound for \eqref{phi err}, we can construct an error bound bound for
\begin{equation} \label{square bound}

\end{equation}
To do so, we recall that for any $a, b \in \mathbb{R},$
 \begin{equation}
\min\{a, b\} = \frac{a + b - |a-b|}{2}.
\end{equation}
Thus, we can write \eqref{square bound} as
\begin{align}
    \left| \gamma^2(z,A) - \Phi_n^2(z,A) \right|
    % &= \left|

% \begin{align}

\subsection{The Outer Refinement Criteria}

Given a fixed $\epsilon > 0,$ we want to use Colbrook's algorithm, Algorithm \ref{pseudospec}, to efficiently compute the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of a closed and densely-defined linear operator $A.$ To do so, we propose an approach that couples refinement of $\operatorname{Grid} \subset \mathbb{C}$ with our adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR}. In this subsection, we outline the specifics of this coupling.

We begin by fixing a bounded region $\mathcal{D}$ in the complex plane where we want to resolve the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum of $A.$ Let $\mathcal{T}_k$ denote a triangulation of $\mathcal{D}$ at the outer refinement level $k.$ For each triangle $K \in \mathcal{T}_k,$ we denote the barycentre of $K$ by $z_K$ and the diameter of $K$ by 
\begin{equation} \label{diam}
    h_K = \max_{z, w \in K} |z-w|.
\end{equation}
 For Colbrook's algorithm at the outer refinement level $k,$ we take 
\begin{equation} \label{Grid}
    \operatorname{Grid} = \{ z_K : K \in \mathcal{T}_K \}.
\end{equation}
In other words, for each triangle $ K \in \mathcal{T}_k,$ we consider the barycentre $z_K$ and employ Colbrook's algorithm to compute $\Phi_n(z_K, A).$ This setup is illustrated in Figure \ref{Triang}.

Consider a triangle $K \in \mathcal{T}_k$ with barycentre $z_K,$ as illustrated in Figure \ref{Triang}. We want to determine whether the contour representing the boundary of the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum, i.e., the curve $\gamma(z, A) = \epsilon$, passes through this triangle. To do so, we first make use of the following result:
\begin{lemma}[The Resolvent Norm is 1-Lipschitz\footnote{This result is stated as a part of Exercise 1.8 in \cite{Matt}. We developed the proof using arguments similar to the proof of Lemma 3.4 in \cite{proofpaper}.}] \label{lemma}
    Let $\gamma(z,A)$ be defined as in \eqref{var gamma}. Then $\gamma(\,\cdot\,,A)$ is $1$-Lipschitz, that is,
    \begin{equation}
        |\gamma(z,A)-\gamma(w,A)| \leq |z-w|,
        \qquad \forall z,w\in\mathbb{C}.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Recall from \eqref{var gamma} that $\gamma(z, A) = \min\left\{ \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(z)}, \sqrt{\mu^{(2)}(z)} \right\},$ where 
    \begin{align}
    \mu^{(1)}(z) &= \inf_{f \in D(A), \|f\|_H=1} \|(A-zI) f \|_H^2,\\
    \mu^{(2)}(z) &= \inf_{f \in D(A^*), \|f\|_H=1} \|(A-zI)^*f\|_H^2.
    \end{align}
    Notice that 
    \begin{equation}
        (A-zI)f = (A-wI)f + (w-z)f.
    \end{equation}
    Thus, 
    \begin{equation}
        \|(A-zI)f\|_H \le \|(A-wI) f \|_H + \|(w-z) f \|_H.
    \end{equation}
    Taking the infinum over all $f \in D(A)$ such that $\|f\|_H=1,$ we obtain
    \begin{equation} \label{g1w}
        \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(z)} \le \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(w)} + |z-w|.
    \end{equation}
    Interchanging $z$ and $w,$ we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(w)} \le \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(z)} + |z-w|.
    \end{equation}
    Thus, $\sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(\cdot)}$ is 1-Lipschitz:
    \begin{equation}
        \left| \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(z)} - \sqrt{\mu^{(1)}(w)} \right| \le |z-w|.
    \end{equation}
    Similar arguments show that $\sqrt{\mu^{(2)}(z)}$ is 1-Lipschitz. 
% footnote{This proof can be found in the proof of Lemma 3.4 in Christopher Sorg's \textit{Residual SCI Upper Bounds and Lower Witnesses For Koopman Approximate Point Spectra in} $L^p$ \textit{For} $1 < p < \infty:$ \textit{ Extended Version.}}
    Since the minimum of two 1-Lipschitz functions is itself 1-Lipschitz, we have our desired result. 
\end{proof}

Assume that the pseudospectral contour $\gamma(z, A)=\epsilon$ does pass through triangle $K.$ That is, assume that there is a $z_{\epsilon} \in K$ such that $\gamma(z_{\epsilon}, A) = \epsilon.$ Using Lemma \ref{lemma} we have that
\begin{align}
    |\Phi_n(z_K, A) - \epsilon| &\le |\Phi_n(z_K, A) - \gamma(z_k, A)| + |\gamma(z_K, A) - \epsilon |\\
    &= |\Phi_n(z_K, A) - \gamma(z_K, A)| + |\gamma(z_K, A) - \gamma(z_{\epsilon}, A) | \\
    &\le |\Phi_n(z_K, A) - \gamma(z_K, A)| + |z_K - z_{\epsilon}| \\
    &\le |\Phi_n(z_K, A) - \gamma(z_K, A)| + h_K,
\end{align}
where $h_K$ is as defined in \eqref{diam}. Using \eqref{pract bound}, we write
% \begin{equation} \label{necessary}

\begin{equation} \label{necessary}
    |\Phi_n(z_k, A) - \epsilon| \lesssim     \sqrt{|\eta^{(1)}(z_K)|
    +|\eta^{(2)}(z_K)|} + h_K,
\end{equation}
where we have used `$\lesssim $' to indicate that \eqref{pract bound} is a heuristic error bound. With this, we can treat \eqref{necessary} as a necessary condition for the pseudospectral contour $\gamma(z, A) = \epsilon$ to pass through triangle $K.$

% \begin{align}

    | \gamma(w, A) - \Phi_n(z_K, A)| &\le |\gamma(w, A) - \gamma(z_K, A) | + | \gamma(z_K, A) - \Phi_n(z_K, A) |\\

\end{equation}

\subsection{Our Implementation}

In this subsection, we construct our methodology for efficient pseudospectral computations using Colbrook's algorithm. The method couples the inner refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR} with the outer refinement technique of the previous subsection. The main idea behind this coupling is to only solve the auxiliary eigenproblems of Colbrook's algorithm to an accuracy that agrees with the current resolution of the complex plane.

As before, let $\mathcal{T}_k$ denote the triangulation of a bounded region $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbb{C}$ at the outer refinement level $k.$ Let $K$ index each triangle with barycentre $z_K$ and diameter $h_K.$ Furthermore, as in \eqref{Grid}, set $\operatorname{Grid}$ to be the collection of barycentres.

For each $z_K \in \operatorname{Grid},$ we solve for the smallest eigenvalues of the two auxiliary eigenproblems that arise in Colbrook's algorithm, Algorithm \ref{pseudospec}. In particular, we discretize the eigenproblems using the finite element method and employ the adaptive mesh refinement technique of Section \ref{AMR} to adapt the underlying meshes until the error estimates, $\eta^{(1)}(z_K)$ and $\eta^{(2)}(z_K)$ in \eqref{eta ests}, satisfy
\begin{equation} \label{inner crit}
    |\eta^{(1)}(z_K)| \le h_K^2/2, \text{ and } |\eta^{(2)}(z_K)| \le h_K^2/2,
\end{equation}
or until a prescribed maximum number of degrees of freedom has been reached. These stopping criteria have been chosen so that the error incurred from approximating the smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems is of the same order as the $\mathcal{O}(h_K)$ uncertainty in representing the triangle $K$ with its barycentre $z_K.$ In this way, the auxiliary eigenproblems are not solved to a greater accuracy than what is warranted by the resolution of the complex plane. 

Once \eqref{inner crit} has been satisfied, we compute $\Phi_n(z_K, A)$ as in \eqref{approx phi}. Using the DWR corrected eigenvalues, $\mu_{n,c}^{(1)}(z_K)$ and $\mu_{n,c}^{(2)}(z_K)$ in \eqref{corrected}, we also compute the corrected approximation
\begin{equation} \label{phi corr}
    \Phi_{n,c}(z_K, A) = \sqrt{ \min \left\{ \mu_{n,c}^{(1)}(z_K), \mu_{n,c}^{(2)}(z_K) \right\}   }.
\end{equation}

Using the classification criteria of the previous subsection, we next decide how to classify $K.$ If \eqref{case 1} holds, we classify $K$ as lying entirely within the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. If \eqref{case 2} holds, we classify $K$ as lying entirely outside of the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. If \eqref{case 3} holds, the current information available does not allow us to determine whether $K$ is inside or outside the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. As a result, we mark $K$ for refinement. A triangle that has been marked for refinement is refined by connecting the four midpoints of its three edges, resulting in four sub-triangles. 

Once we have iterated over the barycentre of each triangle in $\mathcal{T}_k$, the sub-triangles that result from refinement are used to create the updated triangulation $\mathcal{T}_{k+1}.$ We then iterate over the new set of barycentres and repeat the same classification procedure. Since the sub-triangles have a smaller diameter than the triangles in $\mathcal{T}_k,$ the stopping criterion \eqref{inner crit} for the adaptive mesh refinement technique will be more demanding. In this way, the outer refinement of the complex plane requires increasingly greater accuracy in the inner refinement of the auxiliary eigenproblems. 

This coupling of our outer and inner refinement techniques is summarized in Algorithm \ref{outerloop}. With this approach, the computational effort of Colbrook's algorithm is concentrated near the pseudospectral contour $\gamma(z, A) = \epsilon.$ To implement this approach, we begin with a relatively coarse triangulation of the complex plane together with coarse discretizations of the auxiliary eigenproblems that arise at each barycentre. As we near the pseudospectral contour in the complex plane, both the triangulation of the complex plane and the corresponding discretizations of the auxiliary eigenproblems are refined. We continue this coupled refinement process until every triangle that would otherwise be marked for refinement has its diameter below a prescribed tolerance. At that stage, the remaining triangles are taken to approximate the $\epsilon$-pseudospectral contour. In the next Chapter, we will demonstrate the success of this approach by applying Algorithm \ref{outerloop} to numerous examples.

\section{One-Dimensional Dirac Folding of the Poisson Problem}

For our first example, we consider the following self-adjoint, one-dimensional
Dirac-type operator posed on $\Omega=(0,\pi)$:
\begin{equation} \label{dirac pois}
    A(u, \sigma) = (-i\sigma', -i u'), 
\end{equation}
where  $D(A) = H_0^1(\Omega) \times H^1(\Omega)$ is dense in $L^2(\Omega) \times L^2(\Omega)$ \cite{dirac}.

What is special about this operator is its relation to the Poisson eigenproblem. To see this, consider the eigenproblem $AU = \lambda U,$ where $U = (u, \sigma)^T.$ The two equations are
\begin{align}
-i \sigma' &= \lambda u, \label{eq1}\\
-i u' &= \lambda \sigma. \label{eq2}
\end{align}
For $\lambda \ne 0,$ we can solve for $\sigma$ in the \eqref{eq2} and substitute the result into the \eqref{eq1} to obtain 
\begin{equation} \label{pois}
    -u'' = \lambda^2u.
\end{equation}
Notice that \eqref{pois} is the one-dimensional Poisson eigenproblem with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Thus, 
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \pm n, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots
\end{equation}
Furthermore, \eqref{dirac pois} has a zero eigenvalue. Setting $\lambda = 0$ yields $u'=0$ and $\sigma'=0.$ Since $u$ satsifies Dirichlet boundary conditions, $u=0.$ However, since there are no boundary conditions imposed on $\sigma,$ we can take $\sigma = c$ for any constant $c.$ In this way, $U = (0, c)^T$ is an eigenfunction of \eqref{dirac pois} corresponding to $\lambda = 0.$ 

Since \eqref{dirac pois} is self-adjoint and has a compact resolvent, its spectrum consists only of its eigenvalues (CITE!).
Thus, since \eqref{dirac pois} is normal, its  $\epsilon$-pseudospectum is the collection of $\epsilon$-balls centred at each eigenvalue of \eqref{dirac pois}. 


For the numerical experiment of this section, we use Algorithm \ref{outerloop} to compute the $\epsilon = 0.2$ pseudospectrum of \eqref{dirac pois} both accurately and efficiently. 
To do so, we first formulate the auxiliary eigenproblems needed for Colbrook's algorithm. Since \eqref{dirac pois} is self-adjoint, we can formulate the auxiliary eigenproblems as we did for the Maxwell problem in Section \ref{Matt Max}. That is, we consider a $V_h \subset D(A)$ and seek the smallest eigenvalue $\mu_n(z)$ that satisfies 
\begin{equation} \label{dirac eig}
    a_z(u_h, v_h) = \mu_n(z) m(u_h, v_h),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    &a_z(u, v) \defeq ((A-zI) u, (A-zI) v)_{H},\\
    &m(u, v) \defeq (u, v)_{H}.
\end{align}
With this, the desired approximation of the bound on the distance to the spectrum is
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_n(z, A) = \sqrt{\mu_n(z)}.
\end{equation}


 To implement Algorithm \ref{outerloop}, we consider the region $D = [-0.5, 4.5] \times [-1, 1]$ in the complex plane. For our initial triangulation $\mathcal{T}_0,$ we partition $D$ into a uniform
array of $20 \times 8$ squares with sidelength $0.25$, and subdivide each square into two triangles as in Figure \ref{Triang}.  
  For the initial finite element discretization of \eqref{dirac eig}, we partition $\Omega = (0, \pi)$ into sixteen subintervals and take $V_h \subset D(A)$ to be the corresponding quadratic Lagrange finite element space. 

  For the outer stopping tolerance, which controls the diameter of the triangles in the complex plane, we take $h_{\text{tol}} = 0.01.$ Since \eqref{dirac pois} is self-adjoint, the inner stopping tolerance \eqref{inner crit} becomes
  \begin{equation}
    |\eta(z)| \le h_K^2,
  \end{equation}
  where $\eta(z)$ is the computed DWR error estimate \eqref{DWR} for $\mu_n(z)$ in \eqref{dirac eig}.
