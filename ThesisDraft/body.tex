Although the importance of spectra is beyond question, their reliable computation can be challenging \cite{Forward}. 
In finite dimensions, we compute the spectrum of a matrix, which is equivalent to the set of its eigenvalues. In particular, if
$B \in \mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$, then its spectrum is the following set of points:
\begin{equation} \label{matrix spec}
    \operatorname{Sp}(B) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: \det (B-zI) = 0 \} = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (B-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist} \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity matrix. 
It is tempting to think that this is a straightforward problem. However, as we will see, the computed eigenvalues of non-normal matrices can be unreliable. 

In infinite dimensions, we consider a linear operator $A$ on a Hilbert space $\H,$ whose spectrum is defined by
\begin{equation} \label{SpA}
    \operatorname{Sp}(A) = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: (A-zI)^{-1} \text{ does not exist as a bounded operator on } \H \},
\end{equation}
where $I$ denotes the identity operator. 

Recall that a point $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if there exists a nonzero $u$ such that
\begin{equation}
Au=z u.
\end{equation} 
Equivalently, $z$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ when $A-z I$ is not injective. 
% In finite dimensions, the spectrum of an operator coincides with the set of its eigenvalues. 
In contrast to finite dimensions, the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator need not coincide with the set of its eigenvalues. For example, at some $z \in \mathbb{C},$ $A-zI$ may be injective but not surjective. Thus, the
spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator includes the set of its eigenvalues, but is generally much richer.

As a result, new phenomena are present in the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators that make them much harder to reliably compute. To see this, consider the right-shift operator, 
\begin{equation}\label{right shift}
R(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots),
\end{equation}
that acts on square-summable sequences\footnote{The set of square-summable sequences is
    $\ell^2(\mathbb{N}) = \{z = \{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}: \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |x_n|^2 < \infty\}.$}. This operator's spectrum is the unit disc, but it has no eigenvalues\footnote{For more information on this example, see \Cref{Shift Appendix}.}.

The standard approach for computing the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator is often referred to as the \textit{discretise-then-solve} approach \cite[Chapter~1]{Matt}. 
% \pef{Big Book Principle} 
% \cite[Chapter~1]{Matt}
This approach consists of discretising the operator with a finite-dimensional matrix, computing the spectrum of the discretised operator, and hoping that the spectrum of the discretised operator approaches the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator as the size of the discretisation increases. Despite being a successful approach for many problems, it is fraught with difficulties. If the problem is not discretised very carefully, \textit{spectral pollution} can occur, where a sequence of discretised operators $A_n$ has a corresponding sequence of eigenvalues $\lambda_n \in \Sp{A_n}\!$ such that $ \lambda_n \rightarrow \lambda \notin \Sp{A}$ as $n \rightarrow \infty.$
% the discretised operator has eigenvalues that converge to something not in the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator. \pef{Need to be more precise here: it is a sequence of operators that has a sequence of eigenvalues that converges} 
The approach can also suffer from \textit{spectral invisibility}, where 
there exists a $\lambda \in \Sp{A}$ for which no sequence $\lambda_n \in \Sp{A_n}$ converges to $\lambda$ as $n \rightarrow \infty.$
% discretising the operator causes us to miss parts of the spectrum, even as the size of the discretisation increases. 

Even if the discretisation manages to avoid both spectral pollution and spectral invisibility, there remains a far more catastrophic limitation of the discretise-then-solve approach. In particular, since the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators may include more than just eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, there may be parts of the spectrum that we can never capture with the finite set of eigenvalues that results from discretisation \cite[Section~1.1]{Matt}.

We first aim to exploit the structure of the auxiliary eigenproblems in Colbrook's algorithm in an effort to solve them efficiently. With these estimates, we can both improve the computed approximations in Colbrook's algorithm and drive adaptive refinement of the meshes used to discretise the auxiliary eigenproblems.

Let $\H$ be a Hilbert space with inner product $(\cdot, \cdot)_{\H}$ and norm $\|\cdot \|_{\H}.$
We consider a linear operator $A : D(A) \rightarrow \H$ defined on a subspace $D(A) \subset \H.$ We use $D(A)$ and $R(A)$ to denote the domain and range of $A,$ respectively. Furthermore, we use $N(A)$ to denote the null space, or kernel, of $A.$

We say that $A$ is \textit{boundedly invertible} if there exists a bounded operator $B:\H \rightarrow D(A)$ such that $AB=I$ and $BA = I,$ where $I$ denotes the identity operator. In this case, $B$ is called the \textit{inverse} of $A,$ which we denote by $A^{-1}$ \cite[Appendix~A.3]{Matt}.  For example, consider the operator $A = 2I,$ where $I$ is the identity operator on $\H.$ This operator is boundedly invertible with inverse $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} I.$ 


We say that $A$ is \textit{closed} if and only if it has the following property: whenever $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence in $D(A)$ such that $x_n \rightarrow x$ and $A x_n \rightarrow y,$ then $x \in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$ \cite[Theorem~4.13-3]{krey}.
We note that when $A$ is closed, being boundedly invertible is equivalent to $N(A) = \{0\}$ and $R(A) = \H.$\footnote{When $N(A) =\{0\}$ and $R(A) = \H,$ $A^{-1}$ is defined. Since $A$ is closed so is $A^{-1}.$ Then, the Closed Graph Theorem tells us that $A^{-1}$ is bounded \cite[Theorem~4.13-2]{krey}.}

\section{Dangerous Pitfalls of the Discretise-Then-Solve Approach}\label{pit}

The most common approach for computing the spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator is the discretise-then-solve approach. This approach discretises the infinite-dimensional operator, using, for example, a spectral or finite element method, and computes the eigenvalues of the resulting finite-dimensional matrix. The success of this method hinges on whether the set of computed eigenvalues converges to the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator as the size of the discretisation increases. While such an approach can be successful for many problems, it is known to fail catastrophically for others.

 A common failure mode of the discretise-then-solve approach occurs when the computed spectrum contains values that do not belong to the spectrum of the infinite-dimensional operator. This phenomenon is referred to as spectral pollution, which is formally defined as follows:
\begin{definition}[Spectral Pollution {\cite[Definition~1.1.1]{Matt}}]
    Let $\Gamma_n(A) \subset \mathbb{C}$ denote an approximation of $\Sp{A}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ We say that the sequence of approximations $\{\Gamma_n(A)\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ suffers from spectral pollution if there exists $\lambda \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \Sp{A}$ such that 
    \begin{equation}
        \liminf_{n \rightarrow \infty} \operatorname{dist}(\lambda, \Gamma_n(A))= 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}Spectral pollution is a more dangerous phenomenon than spurious eigenvalues of a fixed discretisation. A spurious eigenvalue of a fixed discretisation may move or disappear as the discretisation is refined. However, spectral pollution can occur when there is a sequence of spurious eigenvalues that converge to a value not in the spectrum. This convergence is deceiving, as it makes the spurious limit seem like a genuine spectral value. 

Spectral invisibility occurs when the discretisation of the infinite-dimensional operator causes us to miss parts of its spectrum, even as the size of the discretisation increases. This failure mode is formally defined as follows:
\begin{definition}[Spectral Invisibility {\cite[Definition~1.1.2]{Matt}}]
    Let $\Gamma_n(A) \subset \mathbb{C}$ denote an approximation of $\Sp{A}$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ We say that the sequence of approximations $\{\Gamma_n(A)\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ suffers from spectral invisibility if there exists $\lambda \in \Sp{A}$ such that 
        \begin{equation}
        \limsup_{n \rightarrow \infty} \operatorname{dist}(\lambda, \Gamma_n(A))> 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Even when the discretise-then-solve approach avoids both spectral pollution and spectral invisibility, there is a fundamental issue that arises when an infinite-dimensional operator is replaced by a finite-dimensional discretisation. For general operators, the \textit{essential spectrum} refers to the $z \in \Sp{A}$ for which $A-zI$ fails to be invertible in an `infinite-dimensional' way\footnote{We omit a formal definition of the essential spectrum because there are at least five technical definitions for non-normal operators. The curious reader can consult \cite[Ch. 8]{Matt}.}. The \textit{discrete spectrum} refers to the $z \in \Sp{A}$ for which $A-zI$ fails to be invertible in a `finite-dimensional' way. 
% Thus, the discrete spectrum can be thought of as the subset of $\Sp{A}$ that contains the eigenvalues of $A$ with finite multiplicity. 
Immediately discretising $A$ with a finite matrix results in a finite, discrete set of eigenvalues. As a result, the discretise-then-solve approach may struggle to capture the essential spectrum of an operator, and to distinguish it from the discrete spectrum.

\subsection{The Maxwell Eigenproblem and Spectral Pollution}\label{Maxwell Sect}


To illustrate the effect of these failure modes, we consider the Maxwell eigenproblem, a famous example for which na\"{i}ve finite element discretisations exhibit spectral pollution. 

The Maxwell eigenproblem describes electromagnetic oscillations in a resonator. The standard problem is posed on a bounded Lipschitz domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3.$ The aim is to find the resonant frequencies $\omega \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ and the nonzero electromagnetic fields $(\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{H}) \in \mathbf{H}_0(\bcurl;\Omega) \times \mathbf{H}(\bcurl;\Omega)$ such that
\begin{subequations}\label{3D Max}

\section{Colbrook's Algorithm for Pseudospectra}



%The pitfalls of the discretise-then-solve approach motivate the development of alternative methods for computing the spectra of infinite-dimensional operators. In recent work, Matthew Colbrook has proposed an approach that avoids these pitfalls by working with the infinite-dimensional operator as far as possible, thereby delaying discretisation \cite{Matt}. In this way, Colbrook shows that when and how discretisation is introduced is crucial to the reliable computation of spectra. 


We now introduce Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra. We focus on his algorithm for pseudospectral computations rather than his algorithm for spectral computations for three main reasons. First, it applies to a much broader class of operators than his algorithm for spectra. Second, it forms the main computational component of his algorithm for computing spectra. Finally, as discussed in \Cref{pseudo sec}, the pseudospectra of an operator are often more informative than the spectrum alone.

In \Cref{ModSec}, we introduce the fundamental tool for computing pseudospectra, the \textit{injection modulus} of an operator. In \Cref{Fold sect}, we use this tool to construct Colbrook's algorithm for pseudospectra. Then, in \Cref{Fix matt}, we specialise the algorithm to the finite element setting. Finally, in \Cref{Matt Max}, we demonstrate the success of Colbrook's algorithm by applying it to the Maxwell eigenproblem. 


\subsection{Characterising the Spectrum with Injection Moduli} \label{ModSec}
Let $A: D(A) \subset \H \rightarrow \H$ be a closed and densely defined linear operator on the Hilbert space $\H$. The first ingredient for Colbrook's algorithm is the \textit{injection modulus} of $A,$ which is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{inject}
    \sigma_{\inf}(A) = \inf\{ \|Ax\|_\H : x \in D(A), \|x\|_\H = 1\}.
\end{equation}
% The injection modulus is a quantitative measure of `how injective' an operator is \cite{Matt}. In this way, it
The injection modulus of an operator is often viewed as a generalisation of the smallest singular value of a finite-dimensional matrix. However, we must be careful not to confuse the injection modulus with the smallest singular value, since infinite-dimensional operators can exhibit behaviours that are quite different from those of finite-dimensional operators \cite[Section 1.2.3]{Matt}. For example, an infinite-dimensional operator $A$ can be injective but have $\sigma_{\inf}(A)=0.$ This may happen when there is a sequence of unit vectors, $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, such that $\|A x_n \|_\H \rightarrow 0$ but there is no nonzero $x \in D(A)$ that satisfies $\|Ax\|_\H=0.$ 
% \pef{So it isn't a quantitative measure of `how injective' an operator is? This seems contradictory.}

One of the main tools in Colbrook's algorithm is the following function of $z 
\in \mathbb{C},$
\begin{equation}
    \gamma(z, A) = \min \left\{ \sigma_{\inf}(A-zI), \sigma_{\inf}(A^* - \overline{z}I) \right\}.
\end{equation}
The following lemma identifies  $\gamma(z, A)$ with the inverse resolvent norm.
% \pef{End sentences before lemmas/theorems/etc.}
\begin{lemma}[Injection Moduli and Resolvent Norms {\cite[Lemma~1.2.5]{Matt}}] \label[lemma]{gamma and norm}
   Let $A$ be a closed and densely defined operator on a Hilbert space $\H.$ We have that
   \begin{equation} \label{gamma}
       \gamma(z, A) = \|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\H)}^{-1},
   \end{equation}
   where we adopt the convention that $\|(A-zI)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\H)}^{-1} = 0$ if $z \in \Sp{A}.$ Furthermore, if $z \notin \Sp{A},$ then $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I).$
\end{lemma}

In finite dimensions, we have that $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I)$ for every $z \in \mathbb{C}.$ However, in infinite dimensions, it is possible to have $z \in \Sp{A}$ and $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) \ne \sigma_{\inf}(A^*-\overline{z}I).$ To see this, consider the right-shift operator as defined in \eqref{right shift}, i.e., the operator $R:\ell^2(\mathbb{N}) \rightarrow \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ defined by $R(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots).$ This operator satisfies $\|Rx\|_{\ell^2(\mathbb{N})} = \|x\|_{\ell^2(\mathbb{N})}.$ Thus, $\sigma_{\inf}(R) = 1.$ Now consider its Hilbert adjoint $R^*,$ which is the left-shift operator $R^*(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots).$ Notice that $R^*(1, 0, \dots) = (0, 0, \dots).$ Hence, $1 = \sigma_{\inf}(R) \ne \sigma_{\inf}(R^*) = 0.$

With \Cref{gamma and norm} in mind, we can define the spectrum of $A$ as follows: 
% \pef{Again, use cleveref}
\begin{equation}\label{gamma Sp}
    \Sp{A} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) = 0\}.
\end{equation}
Similarly, we can define the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum as the following set:
\begin{equation} \label{gamma eps Sp}
    \Spe{A} = \operatorname{Cl} \left(\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) < \epsilon\}\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, to approximate $\Sp{A}$ and $\Spe{A},$ we can approximate $\gamma(z, A).$ This idea underpins Colbrook's algorithms for computing the spectra and pseudospectra of infinite-dimensional operators.


\subsection{Computing Pseudospectra with Operator Folding} \label{Fold sect}

Colbrook's algorithm for computing the pseudospectra of an operator $A$ relies on the characterisation of $\Spe{A}$ using $\gamma(z,A)$ in \eqref{gamma eps Sp}. The key idea is to approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ at chosen test points $z \in \mathbb{C}$ and retain all points for which $\gamma(z, A) < \epsilon.$ To construct this methodology, Colbrook makes the following assumption:
\begin{assumption}[Required Convergence of $\boldsymbol{\Phi_n(z, A)}$ {\cite[Assumption~3.2.1]{Matt}}]\label[assumption]{assump}
    To compute $\Spe{A},$ assume that we can compute functions $\Phi_n(z, A)$ such that 
    \begin{equation}
        \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \sup_{z \in \mathcal{C}} | \Phi_n(z, A) - \gamma(z, A) | = 0 \qquad \Forall \text{ compact } \mathcal{C} \subset \mathbb{C}, 
    \end{equation}
    where the speed of convergence is allowed to depend on $A$ and $\mathcal{C}.$ Furthermore, we assume that $\gamma(z, A) \le \Phi_n(z, A)$ for sufficiently large $n,$ depending on $A$ but not $z.$ 
    
    % (depending on $A$ but not on $z$).
\end{assumption}

% \pef{Clarify that the new assumption here is separability (if I understand correctly).}

To satisfy this assumption, we introduce \textit{operator folding} \cite[Section 3.4]{Matt}. Suppose that $A$ is a closed and densely defined operator on a Hilbert space $\H.$ In addition, we now assume that $\H$ is separable, i.e., that it contains a countable orthonormal basis. Let $\{\mathcal{P}_n\}$ be orthogonal projections such that $\mathcal{P}_n^*\mathcal{P}_n$ strongly converges to the identity as $n \rightarrow \infty$. 
% \pef{You don't need to cite Matt's book on consecutive sentences. Just cite the section on operator folding at the start of the section.} 
The key idea behind operator folding is to approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ using the following relation:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\inf}((A-zI)\mathcal{P}_n^*) =  \sqrt{\sigma_{\inf}(\mathcal{P}_n(A-zI)^*(A-zI)\mathcal{P}_n^*)}.
\end{equation}
That is, we approximate $\gamma(z, A)$ with 
\begin{equation} \label{phi Pn}
    \Phi_n(z, A) = \min \left\{\sigma_{\inf}((A-zI) \mathcal{P}_n^*), \sigma_{\inf}((A-zI)^*\mathcal{P}_n^*) \right\},
\end{equation}
which satisfies \Cref{assump} under suitable conditions on $D(A)$ and $D(A^*)$ \cite[Chapter~3]{kato}. 

To compute $\Phi_n(z, A)$ in \eqref{phi Pn}, we consider the following quadratic forms:
\begin{align}
    Q_{z, A}^{(1)}(u, v)&= ((A-zI)u, (A-zI)v)_{\H}, &\quad u, v &\in D(A),\label{Q1}\\
    Q_{z, A}^{(2)}(u, v) &= ((A-zI)^*u, (A-zI)^*v)_{\H}, &\qquad u, v &\in D(A^*),\label{Q2}
\end{align}
which induce the Rayleigh quotients
\begin{align}
\mathcal{R}_{z, A}^{(1)}(u) &= \frac{Q_{z, A}^{(1)}(u, u)}{(u, u)_\H} = \frac{\|(A-zI)u\|_\H^2}{\|u\|_\H^2}, \label{R1}\\
\mathcal{R}_{z, A}^{(2)}(u) &= \frac{Q_{z, A}^{(2)}(u, u)}{(u, u)_\H} = \frac{\|(A-zI)^*u\|_\H^2}{\|u\|_\H^2}.\label{R2}
\end{align}
These Rayleigh quotients can be used to characterise the injection modulus of $A-zI$ and $(A - zI)^*$ in the following way:

    \langle Lu, v \rangle_{V^*, V} \defeq (\nabla u, \nabla v)_{L^2(\Omega)}, \qquad \Forall u, v \in V,

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \langle T^{\dagger}f, u \rangle_{X^*, X} = \langle f, Tu \rangle_{Y^*, Y}, \qquad \Forall f \in Y^*, \, \Forall u \in X.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
   \langle J_\H u, f \rangle_{\H^{**}, \H^*} = \langle f, u \rangle_{\H^*, \H} \qquad \Forall f \in \H^*, \, \Forall u \in \H. 
\end{equation}
In the complex case, $J_\H$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
   \langle J_\H u, f \rangle_{\H^{**}, \H^*} = \overline{\langle f, u \rangle_{\H^*, \H}} \qquad \Forall f \in \H^*, \, \Forall u \in \H. 
\end{equation}

    &= \bigl(M^{-1}(L-zM)u,\,
             M^{-1}(L-zM)v\bigr)_\H \\
    &= \left\langle
        (L-zM)u,\,
        M^{-1}(L-zM)v

    &= \left\langle
        (M^{-1}(L-zM))^{\dagger}(L-zM)u,\,
        v
    \right\rangle_{V^*,V} \\
    &= \left\langle
        (L-zM)^{\dagger}(M^{-1})^{\dagger}(L-zM)u,\,

    &= \left\langle

 To approximate $\mu^{(1)}(z),$ we consider a finite element space $V_h \subset V$ and write 
\begin{equation}
    \mu_h^{(1)}(z) \defeq \min_{u_h \in V_h\setminus\{0\}} \frac{Q_{z, A}^{(1)}(u_h, u_h)}{m(u_h, u_h)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, $\mu_h^{(1)}(z)$ can be obtained from the smallest eigenvalue of the following eigenproblem: find $\lambda_h \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $u_h \in V_h$ such that 
\begin{equation} \label{aux1}
    Q_{z, A}^{(1)}(u_h, v_h) = \lambda_h m(u_h, v_h) \qquad \Forall v_h \in V_h.
\end{equation}
Similarly, to approximate $\mu^{(2)}(z),$ we introduce the finite element space $W_h \subset W$ and write
\begin{equation}
    \mu_h^{(2)}(z) \defeq \min_{u_h \in W_h\setminus\{0\}} \frac{Q_{z, A}^{(2)}(u_h, u_h)}{m(u_h, u_h)}.
\end{equation} 
Thus, $\mu_h^{(2)}(z)$ can be obtained from the smallest eigenvalue of the following eigenproblem: find $\lambda_h \in \mathbb{R}$ and nonzero $u_h \in W_h$ such that 
\begin{equation} \label{aux2}
    Q_{z, A}^{(2)}(u_h, w_h) = \lambda_h m(u_h, w_h) \qquad \Forall w_h \in W_h.
\end{equation}

The advantage of formulating our approximations in this way is that the sesquilinear representations of 
$Q_{z, A}^{(1)}(u_h, v_h),$  $Q_{z, A}^{(2)}(u_h, v_h),$ and $m(u_h, v_h)$ can be assembled directly in Firedrake. As a result, we can employ Firedrake and SLEPc to compute the smallest eigenvalues, $\mu_h^{(1)}(z)$ and $\mu_h^{(2)}(z),$ of the auxiliary eigenproblems, \eqref{aux1} and \eqref{aux2}, respectively. In doing so, our approximation of $\gamma(z, A)$ is
\begin{equation} \label{approx phi}
    \Phi_h(z, A) = \sqrt{ \min \left\{ \mu_h^{(1)}(z), \mu_h^{(2)}(z) \right\} }.
\end{equation}
Here, we use the subscript $h,$ rather than Colbrook's index $n$ in \Cref{old pseudospec}, to emphasise that the approximation of $\gamma(z, A)$ is obtained using the finite element method. Thus, $\Phi_h(z, A)$ is to be viewed as the finite element realisation of Colbrook's $\Phi_n(z, A).$ In this new setting, the limit $h \rightarrow 0$ plays the role of $n \rightarrow \infty$ in Colbrook's abstract formulation.
Following this line of thinking, \Cref{pseudospec} reformulates Colbrook's algorithm, \Cref{old pseudospec}, in our finite element setting.

Before moving on, we emphasise that our finite element formulation of Colbrook's algorithm applies to operators, $L : V \rightarrow \H^*$ and $S: W \rightarrow \H^*,$ rather than to the more general operators, $L:V \rightarrow V^*$ and $S:W\rightarrow W^*.$ This restriction allows us to work with the Riesz map $M : \H \rightarrow \H^*$ and its inverse $M^{-1}:\H^* \rightarrow \H.$  As a result, our formulation of Colbrook's algorithm is limited to densely defined operators on $\H$ whose variational realisations map into $\H^*.$ All of the operators considered in this work satisfy this requirement. \subsection{Adaptive Finite Element Methods} 

Since the finite element methodology provides an approximation to the solution of an underlying infinite-dimensional variational problem, we may want to measure, control, and effectively minimise the approximation error \cite{Oden}. To do so, we turn to \textit{a posteriori} error estimates. Given a computed solution, \textit{a posteriori} error estimates provide an estimate of the error in that particular approximation. These error estimates are often used to determine how the current discretisation should be modified to reduce the error in the corresponding approximation.

This idea gives rise to adaptive finite-element methods (AFEMs), which are techniques for choosing a finite-dimensional space, $V_h,$ that is optimal with respect to computational complexity and accuracy. This is typically done by adaptively refining the mesh used to construct $V_h$ in regions where the error estimate is large, giving rise to adaptive mesh refinement techniques (AMRs) \cite[Section~8.6]{Scott}.
These adaptive mesh refinement techniques are particularly useful when the error is concentrated in certain regions of the domain\footnote{For example, the error may be concentrated in regions where a solution exhibits singularities.}. In this case, choosing a fine uniform mesh may be a waste of computational effort. In contrast, adaptive mesh techniques aim to refine the mesh only where necessary, making them useful tools for improving the accuracy and efficiency of computed approximations.



When applying the finite element methodology to solve a problem arising in application, scientists and engineers are often interested in a derived quantity of interest, rather than in the solution field over the whole domain \cite{Bangerth, Becker, Heuveline}.

This idea gives rise to the DWR method, which provides \textit{a posteriori} error estimates for prescribed quantities of interest.

\subsection{The DWR Method for the Eigenvalue Error}

% The DWR method was established by Rolf Rannacher and Ronald Becker, amoungst others, in the 1900s and early 2000s. A comprehensive review of its development can be found in \cite{Becker}. 

To illustrate the ideas underlying the DWR method, we begin in an abstract setting. 
Let $u$ denote the solution of the underlying infinite-dimensional problem, often referred to as the \textit{primal problem}. Let $J(\cdot)$ be a scalar function used to compute the quantity of interest $J(u).$ When we discretise the primal problem with the finite element method, we obtain a solution approximation, $u_h,$ and the corresponding approximation of the quantity of interest, $J(u_h).$ The DWR method aims to both estimate and control the error in the computed quantity of interest,
\begin{equation}
    |J(u) - J(u_h)|.
\end{equation}

 
To estimate this error, the DWR method embeds the given problem into the framework of optimal control \cite{Becker}. To do so, the method introduces an associated \textit{dual problem} that depends on the chosen quantity of interest. Using the solution of the dual problem, the method provides an error estimate in the form of a primal residual weighted by a `sensitivity parameter' obtained from the dual solution.  In this way, the dual solution is used to describe sensitivity of the quantity of interest to errors in the computed primal solution. This allows for mesh adaptation driven by the error in the computed quantity of interest rather than by the error in the computed primal solution \cite{Becker}.


% In this way, the dual solution is used to describe sensitivity of the quantity of interest to errors in the computed primal solution, thereby allowing for mesh adaptation driven by the error in the computed quantity of interest rather than by the error in the computed primal solution \cite{Becker}.

We now specialise the DWR framework to eigenproblems, taking the eigenvalue itself as the chosen quantity of interest. 
This application of DWR was first proposed by Vincent Heuveline and Rolf Rannacher in \cite{Heuveline}. 
% \pef{This was first proposed by Heuveline \& Rannacher \dots}
Following their approach, we consider a subspace $V$ of a Hilbert space $\H,$ where the embedding $V  \hookrightarrow \H$ is compact\footnote{
Let $X$ and $Y$ be normed spaces. The linear operator $T$ is compact if and only if it maps every bounded sequence $\{x_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in $X$ onto a sequence  $\{Tx_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in $Y$ that has a convergent subsequence. The embedding $V  \hookrightarrow \H$ is compact when the embedding map from $V$ to $\H$ is compact.}. Let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{M}$ be linear operators on $V.$
 We seek a  $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and nonzero $u \in V$ such that 
\begin{equation} \label{eig prob}
    \mathcal{A}u = \lambda \mathcal{M} u,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is assumed to be self-adjoint and positive semidefinite  \cite{Bangerth}. 

% To do so, we consider a subset $V$ of the Hilbert space $H$ (DO WE NEED EMBEDDING?DENSITY?). Denoting the antidual of $V$ by $V^{\times},$ we consider the linear operators $V, M: V \rightarrow V^{\times}.$

As usual, we prefer the variational formulation
\begin{equation} \label{var eig}
    a(u, v) = \lambda m(u, v) \qquad \Forall v \in V,
\end{equation}
% where 
% \begin{align} 
%    a(\cdot, \cdot) &= \langle A \cdot, \cdot\rangle_{V^{\times}, V},\\
%    m(\cdot, \cdot) &= \langle M\cdot, \cdot\rangle_{V^{\times}, V}.
% \end{align}
% Here, $\langle \cdot, \cdot\rangle_{V^{\times}, V}$ denotes the action of an element in $V^{\times}$ in the first slot, on an element in $V$ in the second slot \cite{umberto}. 
where $a(\cdot, \cdot)$ and $m(\cdot, \cdot)$ are the sesquilinear forms induced by $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{M},$ respectively.
Furthermore, all eigenfunctions that satisfy \eqref{var eig} are assumed to be normalised by $m(u, u) = 1.$

To discretise this eigenproblem, we introduce the finite-dimensional subspace $V_h \subset V$ and seek $\lambda_h \in \mathbb{C}$ and nonzero $u_h \in V_h$ that satisfy
\begin{equation} \label{gal eig}
    a(u_h, v_h) = \lambda_h m(u_h, v_h) \qquad \Forall v_h \in V_h,
\end{equation}
where it is assumed that $m(u_h, u_h) = 1.$
With this, our goal is to control the error in $\lambda_h$. In other words, the quantity of interest is $J\left((u_h, \lambda_h)\right) = \lambda_h.$


As previously discussed, the DWR method introduces a dual problem whose solution is used to describe the sensitivity of the quantity of interest to errors in the primal solution. For a general eigenvalue problem, the dual problem takes the form of the adjoint eigenproblem:
\begin{equation} \label{dual eig}
    \mathcal{A}^* u^* = \lambda^*\mathcal{M}u^*, 
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}^*$ denotes the Hilbert adjoint of $\mathcal{A}$. In the present setting, however, the auxiliary eigenproblems we consider in \Cref{pseudospec} are symmetric. When considering symmetric eigenproblems, the primal and dual eigenproblems coincide. This leads to the following symmetric version of the DWR error estimate, which is stated and proved in \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}:
\begin{corollary}[DWR Method for a Symmetric Eigenproblem \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}] \label{LemDWR}
    Consider an eigenpair $(u_h, \lambda_h) \in V_h \times \mathbb{R}$ that solves \eqref{gal eig}. 
    Define the primal eigenvalue residual as
    \begin{equation} \label{resid}
        \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(\cdot) \defeq a(u_h, \cdot) - \lambda_h m(u_h, \cdot).
    \end{equation}
    We have the error representation
    \begin{equation} \label{DWR}
        (\lambda - \lambda_h)(1 - \xi_h) = \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h),
    \end{equation}
    for an arbitrary $\varphi_h \in V_h,$ with the remainder term
    \begin{equation} \label{remain}
        \xi_h = \frac{1}{2} m(u-u_h, u-u_h).
    \end{equation}

\end{corollary}

% \footnote{In \cite{Bangerth} and \cite{Heuveline}, the general result for non-symmetric eigenproblems is formulated in the Hilbert-space setting, where $A$ and $M$ map from $V$ to $V,$ as opposed to the current antidual setting, in which $A$ and $M$ map from $V$ to its antidual $V^{\times}$. Fortunately, the result and its proof are expressed in terms of the associated variational forms of the eigenproblems, which take the same form in the Hilbert-space and antidual settings. Hence, the result carries over to the current setting unchanged.}:

With this result, we can estimate the error in approximating an eigenvalue of \eqref{eig prob} with an eigenvalue of \eqref{gal eig}. Furthermore, we can localise the global error estimate \eqref{DWR} to obtain local error contributions for each cell of the mesh that underlies the current discretisation. With this in mind, the localisation of \eqref{DWR} can be used to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique. 




\subsection{Localisation of Error Estimates}

% To formulate an adaptive refinement technique, we require local error estimates that describe the contribution that each cell of the current mesh makes to the error in the quantity of interest. These local error estimates are obtained via a localisation procedure applied to \textit{a posteriori} error estimates, such as \eqref{DWR}.

A common approach for localising DWR error estimates is to decompose the integrals appearing in the error estimate over the cells of the mesh and to integrate by parts on each cell. This results in a sum of residual contributions from both the cells and the facets of the underlying mesh.  For worked examples of this localisation procedure, the curious reader may consult \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline, logg, joe}. 

% This results in a sum of error contributions for each cell of the mesh.
% This results in a sum of residual contributions from both the cells and the facets of the mesh.
 % Since interior cell facets are shared by two cells, it is common practice to equally distribute the interior facet contributions between adjacent cells.
 % As a result, the practical use of DWR error control was limited to expert users \cite{logg}.

Traditionally, this localisation procedure had to be performed by hand on a problem-by-problem basis. Consequently, the local error indicators required for adaptive mesh refinement had to be re-derived and implemented for each new problem of interest. This difficulty was addressed by Marie Rognes and Anders Logg, who devised a method for automating the localisation of DWR error estimates \cite{logg}. 


Let $\mathcal{T}_h$ denote the mesh underlying the current discretisation, let $T$ index each cell of the mesh, and let $\partial T$ denote the boundary of $T.$ In \cite{logg}, Rognes and Logg noticed that for a large class of problems, the localisation procedure described above takes a specific form. In particular, they noticed that localisation results in an error estimate that can be written as a sum of contributions from the cells and the facets of the mesh. In the present setting, this observation yields the following localisation of \eqref{DWR}:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \{ (R(u_h, \lambda_h), u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r(u_h, \lambda_h), u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)}\},
\end{equation}
where $R(u_h, \lambda_h)$ and $r(u_h, \lambda_h)$ denote the localised cell and facet residuals, respectively \cite{joe}. Here, $(\cdot, \cdot)_{L^2(T)}$ and $(\cdot, \cdot)_{L^2(\partial T)}$ denote the $L^2$ inner product, where the integration is performed over $T$ and $\partial T,$ respectively. 

With this observation, Rognes and Logg show that by solving local problems on each cell $T$ of the mesh, we can compute piecewise polynomial approximations $R_T$ and $r_{\partial T}$ to $R(u_h, \lambda_h) |_T$ and $r(u_h, \lambda_h)|_{\partial T},$ respectively \cite{logg}. In doing so, the primal residual can be approximated via
\begin{equation}
    \rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u - \varphi_h)  \approx  \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \{ (R_T, u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r_{\partial T}, u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)}\},
\end{equation}
which yields the approximate local error indicators
\begin{equation} \label{local eta}
    \eta_T \defeq |  (R_T, u - \varphi_h)_{L^2(T)} + (r_{\partial T}, u-\varphi_h)_{L^2(\partial T)} |.
\end{equation}
These local error indicators can be used to indicate the regions of the mesh that contribute the most to the error in the desired quantity of interest. As a result, they can be used to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique based on the error in the desired quantity of interest. 

Rognes and Logg's automated localisation procedure was implemented in Firedrake as a part of Joseph Flood's Master's thesis \cite{joe}. This implementation has since been further developed by Patrick Farrell and Pablo Brubeck \cite{pablo}. For this project, we build upon the existing implementation and adapt it to the auxiliary eigenproblems arising in \Cref{pseudospec}. We therefore omit the details of the local problems used to obtain \eqref{local eta} and refer the reader to \cite{joe, logg, pablo} for further details on the localisation procedure and its implementation.

Two common choices for $\varphi_h$ are $\varphi_h \defeq u_h$ and $\varphi_h  \defeq I_h u,$ where $I_hu$ denotes the interpolation of $u$ onto $V_h.$ These choices give the weights $u - u_h$ and $u - I_h u$ in \eqref{DWR} and \eqref{local eta}, respectively. The first represents the discretisation error in the eigenfunction, while the second represents the component of $u$ that is not represented by its interpolant in $V_h.$ In \cite{balance_errs}, it is shown that choosing $\varphi_h \defeq u_h$ allows for the separation of the error incurred by discretisation and the error incurred from inexactly solving \eqref{gal eig}. As a result, we adopt the first choice and take $u- \varphi_h = u - u_h$ in our error estimates\footnote{We note that both choices were tested numerically and produced similar results.}.

To compute either $u-u_h$ or $u - I_h u$, we need the eigenfunction $u$ of the infinite-dimensional problem \eqref{eig prob}. In practice, we do not have access to $u.$ If we did, there would be no need to approximate it. Thus, for the practical computation of \eqref{DWR} and \eqref{local eta}, we have to approximate the terms involving $u.$ In particular, we have to approximate $e \defeq u-u_h$ in the remainder term \eqref{remain}, as well as $e_h \defeq u - \varphi_h$ in \eqref{DWR} and \eqref{local eta}. 

There are many strategies for approximating $e$ and $e_h$, each of which is discussed in \cite{Bangerth, Heuveline}. The most common approaches approximate $u$ in an `enriched' finite element space, $V_h^+,$  that is obtained by increasing the degree of the piecewise polynomial basis used to construct $V_h.$ To obtain an `enriched' approximation $u_h^+$ to $u$ in $V_h^+,$ there are two common approaches. We discuss both in turn:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global Higher-Order Approximation.}
    The simplest approach is to solve for $u_h^+$ by solving \eqref{gal eig} with the choice of $V_h^+$ instead of $V_h.$ An advantage of this approach is that it is easy to implement in Firedrake, which takes care of the finite element assembly, solvers, and interpolation required for $\varphi_h = I_h u_h^+.$ A disadvantage is the cost of re-solving \eqref{gal eig} in a more expensive space. 
    \item \textbf{Local Higher-Order Approximation.}
    An alternative approach is to use patch-wise extrapolation to approximate $u_h^+$ using $u_h,$ without having to re-solve \eqref{gal eig}. Rognes and Logg describe a least-squares implementation of this approach in \cite{logg}. 
\end{enumerate}
Following Flood, Farrell, and Brubeck, we choose the first of the two approaches because of its straightforward implementation in Firedrake \cite{joe, pablo}. Furthermore, the patch-wise extrapolation required for the second approach tends to produce poorer error estimates and is not yet readily available in Firedrake.

With the choices $\varphi_h = u_h$ and $u \approx u_h^+,$ we obtain the following computable DWR eigenvalue error estimate for the eigenvalue error in \eqref{DWR}:
\begin{equation} \label{new DWR}
  \eta \defeq \frac{\rho(u_h, \lambda_h)(u_h^+ - u_h)}{1 - \frac{1}{2} m(u_h^+ - u_h, u_h^+ - u_h)}.
\end{equation}
We use \eqref{new DWR} and its localisation to drive an adaptive mesh refinement technique. We develop this technique in the next chapter.

In the first subsection, we explore the needed assumptions for the successful application of the DWR error estimate in \eqref{DWR} to the auxiliary eigenproblems in \Cref{pseudospec}. In the second subsection, we discuss our adaptation of \eqref{new DWR} to account for eigenvalues with multiplicity greater than one. Finally, in the last three subsections, we outline our methodology for pairing DWR with \Cref{pseudospec} and demonstrate its success when applied to the Maxwell eigenproblem. 


\subsection{The Needed Assumptions}\label{ASSUMPSect}

The DWR error estimate in \eqref{DWR} is formulated for infinite-dimensional eigenproblems as opposed to infinite-dimensional spectral computations. In particular, \eqref{DWR} is derived under the assumption that the desired eigenvalue, $\lambda,$ has an associated eigenfunction $u.$ As a result, the error estimate requires an approximation of the eigenvalue, $\lambda_h,$ and an approximation of the corresponding discrete eigenfunction, $u_h$. 

In \Cref{pseudospec}, the approximate bounds on the distance to the spectrum, $\Phi_h(z, A),$ are obtained by approximating the bottom of the spectrum of the two folded operators $(A-zI)^*(A-zI)$ and $(A-zI)(A-zI)^*.$ There is no requirement that these spectral bottoms be eigenvalues of finite multiplicity with associated eigenfunctions. For example, there can be values of $z$ in the essential spectrum of an operator $A$ for which $\|(A-zI)x_n\|_{\H} \rightarrow 0,$ $\|x_n\|_{\H} = 1,$ and $\{x_n\}_{n\in \mathbb{N}} \subset D(A)$ converges weakly to zero\footnote{Such a sequence is called a \textit{Weyl sequence} and can be used to characterise the essential spectrum of an operator. For more information, see \cite[Ch.\ 8]{Matt}.} \cite[Section 8.1.3]{Matt}. 

With this in mind, in this dissertation, we restrict our attention to operators for which the bottom of the spectrum of each corresponding folded operator is an isolated eigenvalue with finite multiplicity. The precise conditions on the operator $A$ for which this is the case are beyond the scope of the present work, and are left as a topic of future work. Furthermore, in future work, we aim to extend the DWR framework to account for folded operators whose spectral bottom is an element of the essential spectrum.

Let $j \in \{1, 2\}$ index the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem for a fixed $z\in \operatorname{Grid}$ in \Cref{pseudospec}. For each $j,$ the four steps of our adaptive mesh refinement technique take the following form:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*}.] 
    \item \textbf{Solve:} For a fixed $z \in \operatorname{Grid}$ in \Cref{pseudospec},
    solve the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem on its current finite element space $V_h^{(j)}.$ Retain the smallest eigenvalue $\mu_h^{(j)}(z)$ and the corresponding discrete eigenfunction $u_h^{(j)}(z).$ Construct the enriched space $V_h^{(j), +}$ by increasing the polynomial degree that defines $V_h^{(j)}.$ Solve the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem in the enriched space.
    
    \item \textbf{Estimate:} Solve the least-squares problem \eqref{least} to compute the enriched approximation that best matches $u_h^{(j)}(z).$ Apply the DWR error estimate \eqref{new DWR} to obtain the following global error estimate:

        \eta^{(j)}(z) \approx  \mu^{(j)}(z) - \mu_h^{(j)}(z).

        \mu_{h,c}^{(j)}(z) \defeq \eta^{(j)}(z) + \mu_h^{(j)}(z),

    \item \textbf{Mark:}\footnote{This marking scheme was obtained from \cite{Pslides} where it is said to be simpler than the famous Dörfler marking routine, but performs similarly in practice \cite{Dorf, Pslides}.}

    \begin{equation} \label{mark}
        \eta_T^{(j)}(z) \ge \theta \max_{K \in \mathcal{T}_h^{(j)}} \eta_K^{(j)}(z), \, \text{ for a fixed } \, \theta \in [0,1].
    \end{equation}
    Unless otherwise stated, we take $\theta = 1/2.$

    
    \item \textbf{Refine:} Refine the marked cells, thereby defining a new finite element function space for the $j$-th auxiliary eigenproblem. To refine our marked cells, we employ Netgen's refinement technique, as implemented by Umberto Zerbinati \cite{NGSolve, Schoberl1997, pablo}.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Our Implementation}

We now construct our methodology for efficient pseudospectral computations using \Cref{pseudospec}. The method couples the inner refinement technique of \Cref{AMR} with the outer refinement technique of the previous subsection. The main idea behind this coupling is to only solve the auxiliary eigenproblems of \Cref{pseudospec} to an accuracy that agrees with the current resolution of the complex plane.

As before, let $\mathcal{T}_k$ denote the triangulation of a bounded region $\mathcal{D} \subset \mathbb{C}$ at the outer refinement level $k.$ Let $K$ index each triangle with barycentre $z_K$ and diameter $h_K.$ Furthermore, as in \eqref{Grid}, set $\operatorname{Grid}$ to be the collection of barycentres.
For each $z_K \in \operatorname{Grid},$ we solve for the smallest eigenvalues of the two auxiliary eigenproblems that arise in \Cref{pseudospec}. To do so, we apply the adaptive mesh refinement technique of \Cref{AMR} until the error estimates, $\eta^{(1)}(z_K)$ and $\eta^{(2)}(z_K)$ from \eqref{eta ests}, satisfy
\begin{equation} \label{inner crit}
    |\eta^{(1)}(z_K)| \le h_K^2/2, \text{ and } |\eta^{(2)}(z_K)| \le h_K^2/2,
\end{equation}
or until a prescribed maximum number of degrees of freedom has been reached. These stopping criteria have been chosen so that the error estimate for $\Phi_h(z, A)$ in \eqref{pract bound} is of the same order as the $\mathcal{O}(h_K)$ uncertainty in representing the triangle $K$ with its barycentre $z_K.$
In this way, the auxiliary eigenproblems are not solved to a greater accuracy than what is warranted by the resolution of the complex plane. 

% the error incurred from approximating the smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems is of the same order as the $\mathcal{O}(h_K)$ uncertainty in representing the triangle $K$ with its barycentre $z_K.$ 

Once \eqref{inner crit} has been satisfied, we compute $\Phi_h(z_K, A)$ as in \eqref{approx phi}. Using the DWR-corrected eigenvalues, $\mu_{h,c}^{(1)}(z_K)$ and $\mu_{h,c}^{(2)}(z_K)$ in \eqref{corrected}, we also compute the corrected approximation
\begin{equation} \label{phi corr}
    \Phi_{h,c}(z_K, A) = \sqrt{ \min \left\{ \mu_{h,c}^{(1)}(z_K), \mu_{h,c}^{(2)}(z_K) \right\}   }.
\end{equation}

Using the classification criteria of the previous subsection, we then decide how to classify $K.$ If \eqref{case 1} holds, we classify $K$ as lying entirely within the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. If \eqref{case 2} holds, we classify $K$ as lying entirely outside of the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. If \eqref{case 3} holds, the current information available does not allow us to determine whether $K$ is inside or outside the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum. As a result, we mark $K$ for refinement. A triangle that has been marked for refinement is refined by connecting the midpoints of its three edges, resulting in four sub-triangles. 

Once we have iterated over the barycentre of each triangle in $\mathcal{T}_k$, the sub-triangles that result from refinement are used to create the updated triangulation $\mathcal{T}_{k+1}.$ We then iterate over the new set of barycentres and repeat the same classification procedure. Since the sub-triangles have a smaller diameter than the triangles in $\mathcal{T}_k,$ the stopping criterion \eqref{inner crit} for the adaptive mesh refinement technique will be more demanding. In this way, the outer refinement of the complex plane requires increasingly greater accuracy in the inner refinement of the auxiliary eigenproblems. 

This coupling of our outer and inner refinement techniques is summarised in \Cref{outerloop}. With this approach, the computational effort of \Cref{pseudospec} is concentrated near the pseudospectral contour $\gamma(z, A) = \epsilon.$ To implement this approach, we begin with a relatively coarse triangulation of the complex plane together with coarse discretisations of the auxiliary eigenproblems that arise at each barycentre. As we near the pseudospectral contour, both the triangulation of the complex plane and the corresponding discretisations of the auxiliary eigenproblems are refined. We continue this coupled refinement process until every triangle that would otherwise be marked for refinement has its diameter below a prescribed tolerance. At that stage, the remaining triangles are taken to approximate the $\epsilon$-pseudospectral contour. In the next chapter, we will demonstrate the success of this approach by applying \Cref{outerloop} to numerous examples.

The spectrum of this operator can be characterised using its relation to the one-dimensional Laplacian. To see this, consider the eigenproblem $AU = \lambda U,$ where $U = (u, \sigma)^T\!.$ This yields the following two equations:
\begin{align}

For our numerical experiment, we use \Cref{outerloop} to compute the $\epsilon = 0.2$ pseudospectrum of the Maxwell operator \eqref{max op}. To do so,
we consider the region $\mathcal{D} = [2, 4] \times [-1, 1]$ in the complex plane. For our initial triangulation $\mathcal{T}_0$, we partition $\mathcal{D}$ into a $10 \times 10$ array of squares and subdivide each square into two triangles (see \Cref{Triang}). \end{equation}

\section{The Advection--Diffusion Operator}

For our final example, we consider the following one-dimensional advection--diffusion operator posed on $\Omega = (0, 1)$:
\begin{equation} \label{advect}
    Au = - \nu u'' - u', \qquad \nu \defeq 0.1,
\end{equation}
where $D(A) = \text{H}^2(\Omega) \cap \text{H}_0^1(\Omega)$ is dense in $\H = L^2(\Omega)$.


This operator is the negative of the advection--diffusion operator considered in  \cite[Section 3.12]{Nick}. As a result, its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are 
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \frac{1}{4} \nu^{-1} + \nu n^2 \pi^2, \qquad u(x) = e^{-x/2\nu} \sin(n \pi x), \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots,
\end{equation}
respectively. Furthermore, \cite[Theorem~1]{AdvectPaper} can be used to show that the spectrum of \eqref{advect} consists solely of its eigenvalues. 

The Hilbert-adjoint of \eqref{advect} is 
\begin{equation}
    A^* = - \nu u'' + u',
\end{equation}
where $D(A^*) = D(A).$ Since \eqref{advect} is not self-adjoint,  its $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum need not be a collection of $\epsilon$-balls centred at its eigenvalues. In fact, in \cite[Section 3.12]{Nick}, it is shown that the pseudospectra of \eqref{advect} fill the region in the $\lambda$-plane bounded by the parabola $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = \nu (\operatorname{Im}(\lambda))^2.$ 

For the numerical experiment of this section, we use \Cref{outerloop} to compute the $\epsilon =0.5$ pseudospectrum of \eqref{advect}. To do so, we formulate the auxiliary eigenproblems needed for \Cref{pseudospec} as in \Cref{Fix matt}. That is, we consider $V_h \in D(A), $ and approximate the smallest eigenvalues of \eqref{aux1} and \eqref{aux2}. Then, we compute $\Phi_h(z, A)$ as in \eqref{approx phi}.

To implement \Cref{outerloop}, we consider the region $\mathcal{D} = [0, 15] \times [-10, 10]$ in the complex plane. For our initial triangulation $\mathcal{T}_0,$ we partition $\mathcal{D}$ into an array of $18 \times 24$ squares and subdvide each square into two triangles (see \Cref{Triang}). For the outer diameter tolerance, we take $h_{\text{tol}} = 0.01.$

For the initial finite element discretisation, we partition $\Omega = (0, 1)$ into sixteen subintervals and take $V_h \subset D(A)$ to be the cubic Hermite finite element space. To implement the adaptive mesh refinement technique in \Cref{outerloop}, we introduce the enriched space $V_h^+$, obtained by increasing the degree of $V_h$ by one. To solve for the smallest eigenvalues of the auxiliary eigenproblems at each barycentre $z_K$ of a triangle $K \in \mathcal{T}_K,$ we employ SLEPc's shift-and-invert Krylov--Schur method \cite{slep}. We then compute the DWR error estimates \eqref{eta ests} for each eigenvalue. 

 The current implementation of Rognes and Logg's localisation procedure in Firedrake is not suited for the fourth-order auxiliary eigenproblems that arise from folding a second-order operator, such as \eqref{advect} \cite{logg}. Thus, for this example, we employ uniform mesh refinement until the stopping criteria \eqref{inner crit} are satisfied.

In this dissertation, we have developed a method for accurate and efficient infinite-dimensional pseudospectral computations. To do so, we built on Colbrook's algorithm for computing pseudospectra. This algorithm characterises the pseudospectrum of a closed and densely defined linear operator $A$ using the smallest spectral values of the folded operators $(A-zI)^*(A-zI)$ and $(A-zI)(A-zI)^*.$ In doing so, it avoids discretising the operator $A$ directly. Instead, it discretises the auxiliary eigenproblems that characterise the smallest spectral values of the folded operators. This delayed discretisation underpins the success of Colbrook's algorithm, which avoids the common pitfalls of the standard discretise-then-solve approach.


After exploring the abstract formulation of Colbrook's algorithm, we reformulated it for a variational setting in which the auxiliary eigenproblems are discretised with the finite element method. We then demonstrated the success of this approach by comparing it to the standard discretise-then-solve approach for the Maxwell eigenproblem.

To do so, we first used the DWR eigenvalue error estimate to derive an error estimate for Colbrook's pseudospectral computations. This estimate was used to formulate stopping criteria for our adaptive mesh refinement technique based on the desired accuracy of Colbrook's pseudospectral computations.

We then developed a method for adaptively selecting the test points in Colbrook's algorithm. We took these test points to be the barycentres of a triangulation of the complex plane. For each triangle, we formulated criteria for deciding whether the triangle lies inside or outside the desired $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum, or whether the triangle should be further refined. These classification criteria depend on both the diameter of the triangle and the DWR error estimate for Colbrook's pseudospectral computations. We therefore chose the stopping criterion for each adaptive eigensolve based on the diameter of the corresponding triangle. In this way, our methodology avoids solving the auxiliary eigenproblems to a greater accuracy than what is warranted by the current resolution of the complex plane.
